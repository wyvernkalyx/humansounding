# What You're Calling Culture Drift Is Four Operational Failures

The scaffolding argument is settled — co-location supplied information diffusion, norm calibration, and cheap trust as unpriced byproducts, and orgs that never built them deliberately found out. Everyone knows this. The unexamined case is the companies that *did* build deliberately and drifted anyway. In nearly every one I've looked at, the cause wasn't proximity. It was four specific failures, all fixable, none of which require a lease.

**Writing cultures fail on the read side.** Every playbook mandates writing; almost none mandate reading. Volume goes up, calendars don't change, documents become artifacts of having-done-the-process, and a year later you have a wiki nobody trusts. The diagnostic isn't "do we write things down" — it's whether a competent person can reconstruct why a decision was made, by whom, and what was rejected, eighteen months later, without asking anyone. Most heavy-docs orgs fail this, because they write proposals and status rather than provenance. Proposals persuade, then get abandoned on approval. The useful residue is boring: decision, owner, rejected alternatives, revisit-by. Two fixes work — the Amazon-style silent read, which is the only mechanism I've seen that makes reading a scheduled obligation rather than a virtue, and assigning ownership of document decay. Untended docs don't stay neutral; one confidently wrong page costs more than ten missing ones.

**Span of control is the unglamorous answer.** Problem detection used to be passive. The manager reading a room was doing real work that appeared on no calendar. Distributed, detection becomes active, scheduled, and effortful — a genuine increase in per-report cost. Almost nobody adjusted. Companies went remote, held spans at ten or twelve, then expressed surprise when managers stopped noticing disengagement four months before a resignation. The manager didn't get worse; the job got harder and headcount didn't move. The fix is denser management or genuinely autonomous flat units, and it's expensive. Worth naming: a fair number of RTO mandates are functionally an attempt to restore passive detection rather than pay for active detection. That's a defensible trade — it's just a real-estate bill paid to avoid a headcount bill.

**Hybrid is a third mode, not a midpoint.** Two people in a room and three on video isn't a hybrid meeting; it's an in-person meeting with observers, and the decisions get made in the ten minutes after the call ends. The only workable versions are coordinated: whole teams on the same days, meetings all-in-room or all-on-video with no mixed states, anchor days chosen for collaborative work rather than scattered to satisfy a badge-swipe metric. Individually-chosen hybrid days produce the worst outcome available — commute cost plus coordination cost, and a two-tier org besides.

**Promotion systems still run on effort proxies.** Distributed work makes output more legible and effort far less legible. That would be an improvement if promotion evaluated output. It mostly evaluates visible reliability, responsiveness, and presence in the right rooms — proxies calibrated when effort was observable. Remote, they degrade unevenly: still functional for people who are loud in Slack or in a favorable time zone, useless for the person quietly holding up a critical service. The process gets noisier while feeling identical from the inside. Then high performers who correctly perceive the system stopped tracking them leave, and it's logged as culture loss. If you went distributed without rewriting leveling criteria and calibration, you moved the meetings and called it a migration.

The through-line: each of these was a cost the office absorbed invisibly. Distributed work doesn't eliminate them, it just moves them onto the budget where you have to look at them. Drift is what happens when you decline to pay and blame the model instead.
