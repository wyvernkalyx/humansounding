# The Meeting Isn't the Problem

If you already run meetings well — agenda as questions, named decider, silent read, decisions captured at the end — and they still go nowhere, more facilitation won't help. A meeting that fails despite good technique is almost always doing a job nobody has named. Find the job first.

## The usual hidden jobs

**Reassurance.** A senior person is anxious and being in the room lowers it. Stated purpose: coordination. Real purpose: presence. Fix it by delivering the reassurance more cheaply — a written update they trust, a dashboard they check themselves.

**Cover.** Decisions need witnesses so nobody owns them alone. The tell: thorough discussion, unremarkable outcome, and later nobody can say who decided.

**A patch over broken information flow.** Two teams meet weekly because there's no shared source of truth. This meeting runs forever until you fix the underlying system.

**Social maintenance.** Genuinely valuable, usually disguised as work. If that's the real value, design for it — a bad status meeting is a worse social event than a good one.

Only one diagnosis — "this is a decision forum, run badly" — responds to the standard advice. The rest need a different intervention entirely.

## The undiscussable

Meetings that circle for an hour usually contain a disagreement nobody will state. Someone thinks the project shouldn't exist. Someone thinks a peer is underperforming. So the group argues about sequencing instead, endlessly, because the surface argument can't resolve without the real one.

Name the shape, not the accusation: *"We've come back to timeline three times and it hasn't moved. I don't think timeline is the disagreement."* If it's interpersonal, take it out of the room — two 1:1s beforehand beat any amount of skilled moderation. And go first with candor; people match what's demonstrated, not what's invited.

## Your own gravity

If you're senior, your opinion doesn't enter the discussion — it ends it. This is invisible from your seat, because it looks like agreement.

Speak last and mean it. Have people write positions down before discussion so you can see the distribution before you contaminate it. Mark the strength of your views explicitly — "weakly held, argue with me" — because otherwise everything reads as firm. When the room converges fast, suspect deference, not consensus.

Worse than deciding openly: performing openness when you've already decided. If it's decided, say so and use the time to pressure-test implementation.

## Killing a meeting you don't own

A political problem, not a process one. Attacking the meeting attacks its creator, so attack the underlying need instead: propose a cheaper replacement, offer to own it, frame it as a trial. *"I'll send a written update Tuesdays covering exactly this — want to drop the sync for a month and see if anything breaks?"* The owner keeps the outcome and loses only the meeting.

If that fails, shrink your surface area. Attend biweekly, send a delegate, get dropped from the half you don't need.

## The ceiling

Individual meeting hygiene has a limit, and most competent people hit it. Past that point, a full calendar is a symptom of structure: unclear decision rights, dependencies that don't match the org chart, no written channel anyone trusts, a norm that nothing counts unless it was said aloud.

If every meeting on your calendar is individually defensible and you're still drowning, the problem isn't the meetings. It's that the organization coordinates through rooms because the alternatives don't work — and nobody fixes that by running a better hour.
