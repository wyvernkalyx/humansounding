**"Hire for attitude, train for skill" is where the conversation starts, not where it ends.**

We all nod at it. Then we go back to filtering on years-in-role because attitude is hard to evidence in a hiring committee and "8 years at a competitor" isn't.

The gap isn't belief. It's assessment.

A few things that have actually moved the needle for me:

**Score the behaviour, not the vibe.** If your attitude signal lives in the unstructured chat at the end, you're measuring rapport. Put it in the scorecard with defined anchors, or it will quietly collapse into "people like me."

**Probe the failure, not the success.** Anyone can narrate a win. Ask about a project that went badly and listen for where the agency sits in the story. Someone who can name their own contribution to a mess without performing contrition is rare and worth a lot.

**Price the ramp honestly.** This is the part the LinkedIn version of the advice skips. A high-potential hire with a skills gap is a real cost — to your senior people, to your delivery dates, to the hire themselves if nobody has bandwidth to coach them. Attitude hires fail most often not because we misjudged the person, but because we didn't have the capacity to support the bet we made.

**Watch what it does to your bar.** "Attitude over experience" can become a rationalisation for hiring cheap and hoping. If the same reasoning keeps producing candidates who are junior, underpaid, and conveniently agreeable, that's not a philosophy — that's a budget.

The teams that get this right aren't the ones that believe in potential hardest. They're the ones that built the scaffolding to convert it.

Where does this break down for you — the assessment, or the support after the offer?
