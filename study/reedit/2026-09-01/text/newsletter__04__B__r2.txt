# WORK IN PROGRESS

**Issue #47 — "You Named It. Now What?"**

---

**The main point, up front: insight is not an intervention.**

You can name your mismatch in one sentence. You know burnout is an organizational problem wearing a personal costume. And you still wake at 5:40 with your jaw clenched — because knowing the mechanism of a trap is not the same as being out of it.

That gap persists because insight is cheap and conditions are expensive. Diagnosis costs an honest afternoon. Change costs political capital, income risk, or admitting the last three years were a wrong turn. So people stall in the diagnosis, polishing it. It feels like progress.

The question at this stage isn't *what's wrong*. It's: **what's the smallest change that would actually alter the conditions — and what is it costing me to keep not making it?**

Four things I'd want an experienced reader to have.

---

**1. Efficacy recovers last — and predicts relapse.**

Exhaustion is loud; cynicism makes you unpleasant at standup. But reduced efficacy — the collapsed belief that your work is any good — has the longest half-life. It persists after conditions improve, which means you can be objectively recovered while running on a self-assessment written during your worst month.

That's the relapse mechanism. Return to the same environment still believing you're barely competent, and you will over-function to prove otherwise. You'll take the extra project. It will feel like conscientiousness the entire time.

Rebuild it deliberately: small scoped projects you can finish, written evidence of what went well (your memory is currently an unreliable narrator), feedback from someone whose judgment you trust and who is not you.

**2. Your cynicism was load-bearing.**

When you couldn't reduce the demands, you reduced how much you cared. Rational, and it probably kept you functional. But it doesn't switch off when conditions improve.

So: **re-engagement will feel unsafe before it feels good.** Caring again means exposing yourself to disappointment you're currently insulated from. People misread that discomfort as proof the job is still wrong and leave exactly when recovery was becoming possible. Numb two weeks after real change is a wall coming down slowly. Numb two months after is data.

**3. Prescribe rest against your specific deficit.**

You know a vacation doesn't fix it — effects fade within weeks unless something structural changed while you were gone. The refinement worth having is that "rest" is at least four different things: psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery, and control over your own time.

Match the intervention to the deficit. Stripped of autonomy? A rigidly scheduled wellness regimen is more of the disease. Efficacy shot? Lying still won't touch it — you need mastery, something hard and non-work that you get visibly better at. Undifferentiated "rest more" is why a week off changes nothing.

**4. Sometimes it isn't burnout. It's moral injury.**

If the core problem is being asked to do things you believe are wrong — ship the unsafe thing, deliver the false message, manage someone out on a pretext — then "burnout" is the wrong word and it's harming you.

Burnout vocabulary locates the injury in your capacity: depleted, poor boundaries, needs better recovery. Moral injury locates it in the transgression. Symptoms overlap heavily; treatments don't. **Sleep does not fix guilt. Boundaries do not fix complicity.** The honest options narrow to: stop doing the thing, get authority to change it, or leave.

---

**If you're staying on purpose**

Legitimate strategy. Mortgages are real. But a strategic stay has properties an indefinite drift doesn't:

- **A written trigger.** Not "when things improve" — an observable condition. *If the reorg lands and I still report to X.*
- **A date on the calendar.** Six months is about right. "Sometime next year" is not a date.
- **Preserved optionality.** A stay that consumes your ability to leave has stopped being a strategy.
- **A witness.** Someone who knows the terms and will ask. Alone, we renegotiate with ourselves indefinitely.

---

**This week:** pick the one change you've correctly identified and haven't made. Write down what it's costing you to keep not making it. That number is the actual decision — not the diagnosis you've already finished.
