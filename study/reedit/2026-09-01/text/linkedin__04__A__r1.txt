**"Hire for attitude, train for skill" is the most repeated line in hiring and the least operationalised.**

Everyone agrees with it. Then the shortlist gets built on years-in-seat anyway — because experience is scoreable and attitude isn't, and hiring managers default to whatever they can defend in a debrief.

So the useful question isn't whether attitude matters. It's what you're actually measuring when you claim to measure it.

Three things I've found are worth scoring, because they predict something and you can hear them in an interview:

**How fast they say "I don't know."** Not humility as a personality trait — speed. The lag between not knowing and admitting it is the single best proxy I've found for how someone will behave when a project is going sideways.

**What they do with the correction.** Give real feedback in the interview and watch. Do they metabolise it and adjust, or defend the original position? You're not testing agreeableness. You're testing whether new information changes anything.

**Whether they've thought about the problem before asking about it.** Curiosity that hasn't done any homework is just enthusiasm.

The failure mode is obvious and everyone walks into it anyway: "attitude" collapses into "someone I'd enjoy working with," and you build a team that agrees with you. If your attitude criteria aren't written down before the interview, that's what you're doing — you've just replaced a biased-but-visible filter with a biased-and-invisible one.

Structure the assessment or don't claim you're doing it.
