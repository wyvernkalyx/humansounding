**Issue #47 — Money in Your Twenties: The Decade That Does the Heavy Lifting**

---

Hi friends,

A reader named Priya wrote in last month with a question I've gotten some version of maybe forty times:

> "I'm 26, I make okay money, and I have no idea if I'm doing this right. I have some savings, some debt, a 401(k) I've never looked at. Everyone online says I should be investing in index funds AND paying off debt AND buying a house AND starting a side hustle. Where do I actually start?"

Here's the honest answer: your twenties matter enormously for your financial life, but *not* for the reasons personal finance influencers usually say. It's not because you need to be maxing out three accounts by 25. It's because this is when you build the habits and the baseline that everything else compounds on top of — money habits, yes, but also earning power and a nervous system that doesn't panic every time you check your balance.

So let's do the actual order of operations. Not the aspirational one. The real one.

---

## The Order of Operations

Think of this as a ladder. Don't skip rungs, and don't feel bad about which rung you're on.

**1. A starter emergency fund: $1,000–$2,000.**
Not six months of expenses. Not yet. Just enough that a car repair or a dental bill doesn't become credit card debt. This is the single highest-leverage thing most people in their twenties can do, and it takes months, not years.

**2. Capture your employer's retirement match.**
If your job offers to match, say, 4% of your salary and you're contributing 0%, you are declining a raise. On a $60,000 salary that's $2,400 a year you're leaving behind. Log in this week. It takes eleven minutes.

**3. Kill high-interest debt.**
Anything above roughly 7–8% — credit cards especially, which average north of 20% — gets attacked next. Paying off a 22% credit card is a guaranteed 22% return. No investment offers that.

Two valid approaches: highest interest rate first (saves the most money) or smallest balance first (builds momentum). Pick whichever one you'll actually stick with. The math difference is usually small; the psychology difference is not.

**4. Build the real emergency fund: 3–6 months of essential expenses.**
Essential, not aspirational. Rent, food, insurance, minimum debt payments, transit. Not concert tickets. Keep it in a high-yield savings account where it earns something instead of nothing.

**5. Now invest.**
Retirement accounts first (they're tax-advantaged, which is free money in a different costume), then a regular brokerage account. Broad, low-cost index funds. Automatic monthly contributions. Then — and this is the hard part — leave it alone.

**6. Everything else.**
House down payment, wedding, sabbatical, business. These are goals, not emergencies, and they get their own separate savings buckets.

## The Thing About Compounding Nobody Explains Well

You've seen the charts. Here's the version that actually landed for me:

$200 a month, invested from age 25 to 35 and then *never touched again*, tends to end up worth more at 65 than $200 a month invested from 35 to 65 — three times the total contributions.

I'm not showing you this to make you feel bad if you're 29 and starting now. I'm showing you because it reframes what your twenties are for. You don't need big numbers. You need **early, small, and consistent.** A $100/month automatic transfer that you never think about again will beat a heroic $1,000 deposit you make once and then abandon.

Start small enough that you don't notice. Increase it every time you get a raise.

## Lifestyle Creep: The Quiet Killer

Here's the pattern almost everyone falls into. You get a raise. Within three months, your spending has absorbed it entirely, and you cannot name a single thing that improved.

The fix is embarrassingly simple: **when your income goes up, immediately route a chunk of the increase into savings before you adjust to it.** Got a $400/month raise? Move $200 into automatic investing the same week. You still get a $200 lifestyle upgrade. You just don't get the whole thing.

This isn't about deprivation. Spend money on things you genuinely love. But be ruthless about the things you're paying for out of habit, comparison, or a subscription you forgot existed. Most people have a surprising amount of spending in that third category.

## Your Boring, Enormous Superpower: Earning More

Personal finance content overwhelmingly focuses on cutting spending, because it's easy to write about. But there's a floor to how much you can cut and no ceiling on what you can earn.

In your twenties, your income is likely to grow faster than at any other point in your life — and the base you set now compounds too, since future raises are usually percentages of your current salary. A few things worth taking seriously:

- **Negotiate the offer.** Nearly always. The worst realistic outcome is "we can't move on salary." A $5,000 bump at 25 is worth far more than $5,000 by the time it's been percentage-raised for forty years.
- **Change jobs when it makes sense.** Internal raises tend to lag; external offers tend to reset your market rate.
- **Invest in skills that compound**, not just credentials that expire.

And genuinely: your career is a financial asset. Treat learning, relationships, and reputation as part of your money plan.

---

## Things You Can Safely Ignore Right Now

A short list of things people will make you feel anxious about that mostly don't matter yet:

- **Picking individual stocks.** Broad index funds beat most professionals over time. You're not missing out.
- **Timing the market.** Nobody does this reliably. Contribute on a schedule and ignore the news.
- **Buying a house immediately.** Renting is not "throwing money away." It's paying for flexibility, which in your twenties is often worth a great deal.
- **Crypto, day trading, whatever's currently loud.** If you want exposure, cap it at money you can genuinely afford to lose. Treat it as entertainment spending, not a plan.
- **Being behind.** There is no schedule. There are only next steps.

---

## One Thing To Do This Week

Just one:

**Set up a single automatic transfer.** Any amount — $25, $50, $200 — from checking to a high-yield savings account, timed for the day after payday.

That's it. Automation is the whole trick. It removes willpower from the equation, and willpower is the least reliable financial tool you own.

---

## Reader Question

**"Should I pay off my student loans aggressively or invest?"**

Depends on the rate. Above ~7%, prioritize the loans. Below ~4–5%, investing usually wins mathematically. In between, split the difference or just pick the one that lets you sleep. And if you're in the US, check whether you're eligible for income-driven repayment or forgiveness programs before you throw extra money at federal loans — sometimes aggressive repayment is actively the wrong move.
