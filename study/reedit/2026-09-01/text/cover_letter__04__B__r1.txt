# Cover Letter — School Administrator (Internal / Known Candidate)

I've written this for the version of the reader who has already watched you work: a superintendent, principal, or HR director in your own district who knows your record and doesn't need it recited. The letter therefore spends almost no time on background and almost all of it on what you would actually do in the role.

---

**[Your Name]**
[Phone] | [Email]

[Date]

Dear [Dr./Ms./Mr. Last Name],

I'm writing to formally put my name forward for the [Assistant Principal / Principal] position at [School Name].

You already know my work, so I won't restate it. I'd rather use this letter to tell you what I'd do in the first year.

**Instruction.** My read is that our biggest gap isn't teacher effort—it's the inconsistency between classrooms in [subject/grade band]. I'd tighten the observation cycle to short, frequent visits with same-week feedback, and I'd protect PLC time for looking at student work rather than logistics. Concretely: [specific first move, e.g., a common formative assessment in K–2 literacy by October].

**Culture.** [Name the real problem, e.g., "Referrals from the 7th-grade hallway are running well above the rest of the building."] I'd start by getting clear on whether that's a systems issue or a staffing one before changing any policy. My instinct is that we need a clearer tiered response and better follow-through on Tier 2, not a stricter code of conduct.

**Staff.** We're losing too many teachers in years two and three. I'd formalize the mentoring that currently happens by accident, and I'd make retention conversations a spring priority rather than an August surprise.

**Operations.** The master schedule is a values document. I'd build next year's around protected intervention time and common planning, and I'd be candid with you early about what that costs elsewhere.

Two things you should weigh honestly. First, [name a genuine gap—e.g., "my budget experience is limited to department-level; I'd need real support in year one"]. Second, moving me out of [current role] leaves a hole in [area], and I'd want us to have a plan for that before anything is announced.

I know this building and these families, and I think that's an argument for me rather than against—provided I'm willing to change things, which I am. I'd welcome the chance to talk it through.

Sincerely,

**[Your Name]**

---

### What changed and why

- **No credential recap, no mission-statement flattery.** Both read as padding to someone who already knows you and wrote the mission statement.
- **Past tense became future tense.** When the reader knows your track record, the open question isn't "what have you done?" but "what would you do with more authority?"
- **Specific diagnoses of the school's actual problems.** This is the biggest advantage an internal candidate has—use it. It also signals you'd arrive with judgment, not just enthusiasm.
- **Named a real weakness and a real cost.** With an external reader this can sink you. With someone who already knows your limitations, pretending they don't exist costs you credibility.
- **Much shorter.** Roughly 350 words. Familiar readers skim.

One caution: if the posting goes to a committee that includes outsiders—board members, parent reps, a neighboring principal—assume at least one reader doesn't know you, and add two sentences of grounding at the top.
