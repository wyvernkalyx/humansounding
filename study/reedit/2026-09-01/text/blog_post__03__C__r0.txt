# The Meeting Is Not the Work

Most bad meetings aren't bad because the people in them are bad at meetings. They're bad because nobody decided what the meeting was *for*, and so it defaulted to the only thing a group of people can do without a purpose: talk until the calendar says stop.

Here's how to do better. Not with a new tool or a novelty timer, but with a handful of decisions you make before anyone walks in the room.

## Start by not having the meeting

The highest-leverage meeting improvement is deletion. Before you send an invite, ask what you actually need:

- **Sharing information?** Write it down. A document can be read at 3x the speed of speech, skimmed, searched, and referenced six months later. A status meeting is a document being read aloud badly.
- **Gathering input?** A well-framed async request often gets you better thinking, because people can respond after considering rather than performing in real time.
- **Making a decision, resolving a disagreement, or building shared understanding of something genuinely ambiguous?** Now you have a meeting.

The rough test: if the value comes from the *interaction* — people building on each other, hearing tone, arguing, reading the room — meet. If the value is in the *content*, write.

This also applies to meetings that already exist. Look at your recurring invites. Every one of them was created to solve a problem that may or may not still exist. Give them expiration dates. "This weekly sync ends in eight weeks unless we actively renew it" is a sentence that will save you dozens of hours a year.

## Write the agenda as questions, not topics

Here is a topic: "Q3 roadmap."

Here is a question: "Do we cut the reporting feature to ship onboarding by September 15, or do we move the date?"

The topic agenda produces a meandering discussion where everyone says something about the roadmap and then time runs out. The question agenda produces an answer.

Every agenda item should be phrased as a question to answer or a decision to make, with an owner and a time budget. If you can't write the question, you don't understand what you want yet — and that's useful information. Go figure it out before you spend eight people's hour.

A good agenda looks like:

> **1. (10 min, Priya) Do we ship onboarding without the reporting feature?** Decision. Context in the doc below.
> **2. (15 min, Marcus) What's causing the support ticket spike?** Diagnosis — we need to leave with a hypothesis and an owner.
> **3. (5 min, all) What did we miss?**

Note the third item. Leaving a small slot for "what did we miss" catches the thing that would otherwise derail item one.

## Name the decider before you start

Most meetings that feel endless are meetings where nobody knows who decides. So the group circles, seeking a consensus that isn't coming, until someone with authority gets impatient and unilaterally ends it — which teaches everyone that the previous forty minutes were theater.

Say it out loud at the top: "Priya owns this call. We're here to give her the best possible input, and then she decides." This is not authoritarian. It's the opposite — it makes the input phase real, because everyone knows exactly what it's for and when it closes.

Consensus is a beautiful thing when it happens naturally and an enormous tax when you chase it. Aim for *consent* — people can live with the decision — not agreement.

## Invite fewer people

Every additional attendee costs more than their hour. Groups get slower and more cautious as they grow; people perform for the audience; someone always feels obligated to justify their presence with a comment.

Give everyone a reason to be there: they're deciding, they have information nobody else has, they're doing the work that results, or they need context they genuinely can't get from the notes. "Might be interested" is not a reason. Send them the notes.

If someone's only needed for one item, invite them for that item and let them go. It feels awkward the first time and then it feels obviously correct.

The generous version of this: make it explicitly safe to decline. "Optional for you — I'll send notes" is a gift. So is a culture where people can leave a meeting that turned out not to need them.

## Steal the silent read

If you have a pre-read, do not assume it's been read. It hasn't. People are busy and hope for the best.

The fix, borrowed from Amazon and worth the borrowing: spend the first five to ten minutes of the meeting reading the document, in silence, together. It feels strange for about ninety seconds and then it feels like the most efficient thing you've ever done. Everyone arrives at the discussion with the same context at the same moment. Nobody has to sit through a fifteen-minute verbal recap of something they already read.

Have people comment directly in the doc while they read. By the time the discussion starts, you can see where the disagreement is and go straight there.

## Facilitate, and know that facilitating is a job

The person running the meeting is doing real work: watching the clock, noticing who hasn't spoken, catching when the conversation has stopped producing new information, deciding when to cut something off.

That job is hard to do while also being the most opinionated person in the room. If you're the senior person, consider handing facilitation to someone else — partly for bandwidth, mostly because your opinions will otherwise land like verdicts and end the discussion early.

Some facilitation moves worth having in your pocket:

- **"Let's hear from people who haven't spoken."** The simplest airtime fix there is.
- **"Before we discuss — everyone take two minutes and write your answer down."** Independent thinking before group thinking. Prevents the first speaker from anchoring everyone else, and it's a huge equalizer for people who don't think out loud.
- **"That's a good question and it's not this meeting."** Then write it on the parking lot list, visibly, so the person knows it wasn't discarded.
- **"I want to make sure I understand the disagreement. Sam, you think X because Y. Dana, you think not-X because Z. Is that right?"** Half the time, naming the crux dissolves it. The other half, at least you're arguing about the right thing.
- **"We've got ten minutes left and one decision to make."** Say it before the last minute, not during.

## The last five minutes are the most valuable

The most common way a good meeting produces nothing is that it ends with a warm collective feeling and no record of what happened.

Reserve the final few minutes. Out loud, and in writing, capture:

- **What we decided** (and what we explicitly deferred)
- **Who is doing what by when** — a name and a date, not "the team" and "soon"
- **Who else needs to know**

Read it back before people leave. This is when you discover that two people heard the decision differently, which is much cheaper to find out now than in three weeks.

Send the notes the same day. Short is fine — decisions and owners is enough. The notes are the artifact; the meeting was just how you made them.

## Start on time and end early

Start at the start time. Do not recap for latecomers — it punishes the punctual and rewards the opposite, and people notice which one you're doing.

And when the work is done, end. A meeting scheduled for an hour that finishes in thirty-five minutes has not failed at anything. Giving people back twenty-five minutes is a real gift, and it builds a reputation that means people will actually show up to your next one.

While you're at it: schedule 25 and 50 minutes instead of 30 and 60. Humans need to walk somewhere, refill something, and think for a moment. Back-to-back-to-back calendars produce people who are physically present and cognitively still in the last meeting.

## The underlying idea

Every one of these practices comes from the same place: **a meeting is an expensive, synchronous, hard-to-reverse use of other people's attention, and it should be treated that way.**

You don't need to run meetings perfectly. You just need to be the person who has clearly thought about why everyone is there. That alone puts you in a small minority, and people will notice — usually by saying something like "that was actually a good meeting," in a tone of mild surprise.

Take the compliment. Then look at your calendar and see what else you can delete.
