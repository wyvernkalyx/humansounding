# Remote Work Didn't Kill Your Culture. It Just Stopped Hiding It.

Five years into the great distributed-work experiment, the debate has calcified into two camps shouting past each other. One side says culture requires presence — the hallway conversation, the whiteboard, the shared lunch. The other says culture is about values and trust, and geography is irrelevant.

Both are wrong in an interesting way. Culture isn't destroyed by distance, and it isn't immune to it either. What actually happens is that remote work removes a set of invisible supports that let mediocre cultures *appear* functional. When those supports disappear, the underlying structure either holds or it doesn't.

## First, a working definition

Most conversations about culture go nowhere because nobody defines the term. "Culture" gets used to mean office snacks, a Slack channel for dog photos, and the company's stated values, all in the same paragraph.

Here's a more useful definition: **culture is the pattern of behavior your organization actually rewards, and the way decisions actually get made.**

Not the values on the careers page. The observed pattern. If your stated value is "bias toward action" but the person who ships without three sign-offs gets scolded, your culture is caution. If you say you value work-life balance but every promoted person answers Slack at 11pm, your culture is availability.

This definition matters because it clarifies what remote work does and doesn't affect. Remote work doesn't change what you reward. It changes how *legible* those rewards are, and how information about them spreads.

## What proximity was actually doing

It's worth being honest about the office instead of dismissing it. Physical co-location did three real things, and it did them almost for free.

**Passive information diffusion.** In an office, you overhear things. You learn that the pricing project is stalled, that the CFO is skeptical of the new hire plan, that two teams are duplicating work. Nobody scheduled a meeting to tell you. You absorbed it by being in the room. This is genuinely valuable and remote work destroys it by default.

**Norm calibration.** New employees learn how to behave by watching. How direct can you be in a disagreement? Is it okay to push back on the VP? How polished does a draft need to be before you share it? These norms are almost never written down, and in an office you learn them in weeks by observation. Remotely, you might learn them in a year, or never, or by getting burned.

**Low-stakes trust accumulation.** Trust is built through many small, unremarkable interactions — not through scheduled bonding. Waiting for coffee, walking to a meeting, the two minutes before a call starts. These interactions have almost no content but enormous cumulative effect. You end up knowing someone's temperament, which makes their terse Slack message read as "busy" rather than "angry."

Notice that all three are *byproducts*. Nobody designed them. They happened as a side effect of putting people in a building. This is the crux of the problem: most companies never had to consciously build these mechanisms, so they don't know how, and they don't have the budget line or the management attention allocated to them.

## The scaffolding argument

Here's what I think actually happened when companies went remote.

Organizations with genuinely strong cultures — clear decision rights, explicit norms, managers who could articulate expectations — did fine. Sometimes better than fine. Their culture was encoded in practices, not proximity.

Organizations whose culture ran on osmosis discovered that they had no culture at all, just a set of habits that required a building to sustain. Decision-making that had worked because the right four people sat near each other broke down. Feedback that had happened informally stopped happening. Managers who had "managed" by walking around and getting a vibe were suddenly unable to tell who was struggling.

The office was scaffolding. Take it away and you find out whether anyone built the wall.

The tell is that the loudest return-to-office arguments are usually about *symptoms* — "collaboration is down," "new hires aren't ramping," "we've lost our spark" — rather than mechanisms. Those are real problems. But proximity is an expensive, blunt, and increasingly unpopular treatment for them, and it works by accident rather than design.

## What you have to build deliberately

If you're going to run distributed, you have to consciously rebuild what the building gave you for free. This costs real time and real management attention. Companies that skip this step and then blame remote work are grading their own homework.

**Make writing load-bearing.** Not more writing — *better-placed* writing. The single highest-leverage practice I've seen is requiring that consequential decisions be captured in a short document: what we decided, who decided it, what we considered and rejected, and when we'll revisit. This is unglamorous and most people resist it. It also replaces roughly 80% of what hallway osmosis provided, and unlike osmosis, it's searchable and it reaches people in other time zones.

**Default to public.** Direct messages and private channels are where context goes to die. A reasonable rule: if the conversation would have been audible to others in an office, it should be in a channel others can read. This feels exposed at first. It's also the only way anyone learns anything without being explicitly told.

**Write down the norms nobody writes down.** How fast should you respond to a message? What's the difference between a "@here" and a DM? Is it acceptable to decline a meeting? How much polish does a shared draft need? Every organization has answers to these; almost none have them written. New remote hires are guessing, and guessing wrong is expensive.

**Treat synchronous time as scarce and expensive.** The problem with remote isn't remote — it's that many companies took an office schedule and moved it onto Zoom, producing seven hours of meetings with none of the incidental benefits of being in a room. Meetings should be for things that genuinely require real-time interaction: disagreement, ambiguity, difficult feedback, creative divergence. Status updates are not one of those things.

**Make manager expectations explicit and then resource them.** Remote management is harder, and it is harder in a specific way: you cannot detect problems passively. A manager who could previously read a room now needs regular, structured one-on-ones and has to ask direct questions they used to be able to infer. If you're distributed, your managers need smaller spans of control than you think, and they need to understand that noticing things is now an active task.

## The genuinely hard parts

I don't want to be glib about this. Some things about remote work are hard in ways that practices only partially fix.

**Early-career development is the real one.** People early in their careers learn enormously from ambient observation — watching how a senior person handles a difficult client, how a negotiation actually goes, what "good" looks like at close range. Remote work is much worse at this, and pretending otherwise doesn't help anyone. If you hire juniors and you're distributed, you have to manufacture the exposure: pair them on real work, have them shadow calls they don't strictly need to attend, and build in far more explicit feedback than feels necessary.

**Weak ties atrophy.** You'll maintain your close working relationships fine. What disappears is the acquaintance in another department you'd nod to in the kitchen — the person you'd have asked a quick question. Weak ties are how information crosses organizational boundaries, and they degrade quietly. Cross-team projects and well-designed in-person gatherings help. Random-pairing coffee bots mostly don't, because a forced interaction with a stranger isn't a weak tie, it's an obligation.

**Time zones are underrated as the actual constraint.** Much of what people attribute to "remote" is really "no overlapping hours." A distributed team spread across four hours is a fundamentally different animal from one spread across twelve. Be honest about which one you're building, and design accordingly.

## On in-person time

The most useful reframe I've encountered: stop asking "how many days in the office?" and start asking "what is co-location uniquely good for, and how do we buy exactly that?"

Physical time is exceptionally good at building the trust that makes remote work function afterward. It's excellent for messy early-stage creative work, difficult conversations, and giving new hires a concentrated dose of norm calibration. It's a poor use of expensive travel to have everyone sit in a room reading their laptops and attending the same video calls they'd attend from home.

Which suggests: fewer, longer, better-designed in-person gatherings — with agendas built around the things that actually require bodies in a room — beat a mandatory three-days-a-week policy that reproduces the commute without the benefit.

## The uncomfortable conclusion

Remote work is not culturally neutral, and it's not free. It shifts a set of costs from the physical plant onto management practice and written communication. Companies that pay those costs get a culture that's more explicit, more inclusive of people who aren't in the room, and considerably more durable — because it's encoded in how the organization works rather than in where it sits.

Companies that don't pay them get drift, and then blame the model.

The question worth asking isn't "does remote work harm culture?" It's "was our culture ever anything other than a building?" That's a harder question, and the answer is more useful.
