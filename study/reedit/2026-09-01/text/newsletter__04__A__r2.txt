# WORK IN PROGRESS
### *A newsletter about doing good work without disappearing into it*

**Issue #48 — "The Plateau"**

---

**If you've done everything right and you're still stuck at 70%, it's because you solved a rest problem and you have a conditions problem.**

That's the issue. Everything below is elaboration.

Last time we covered fundamentals. Judging by your replies, most of you didn't need them — you've done the inventory, you know which mismatch is yours, and you're still stuck. So here's what's actually happening.

---

## The 70% plateau

You made the changes. Something genuinely lifted. You got back to roughly 70% of yourself and stopped there for months.

That's not a failed intervention. Exhaustion recovers fastest because it responds to rest. Cynicism and diminished efficacy don't — they respond to *evidence*, accumulated slowly, that the work is worth doing and that you're good at it. The plateau isn't a signal to try harder at recovery. It's a signal that you've moved to a different problem with different treatment.

Related: **the detachment you're trying to eliminate is load-bearing.** It showed up because caring at full volume cost more than you could pay. Dismantle it before the conditions change and you've removed the shock absorber and driven the same road. Be suspicious of the urge to force enthusiasm back. Let it lift on its own schedule — and if it doesn't, it's telling you something accurate.

---

## The mismatches personal tools can't touch

**Workload** and **control** negotiate. There's a version of that conversation that ends with something concretely different on Monday.

**Fairness** and **values** don't. You cannot boundary your way out of a promotion process that rewards visibility over contribution, or time-block around knowing the product is worse than what you sell it as. These yield to three things only: the organization changes, your leverage within it changes, or you leave.

Short list, and uncomfortable. But a lot of the exhaustion in month eight is the exhaustion of applying the wrong tool with increasing force.

---

## Diagnosis as procrastination

Aimed at the people most likely to be reading this: at some point, continuing to analyze your burnout is a way of not acting on it. Retaking the inventory, refining the taxonomy of your own suffering — this feels like progress and is the intellectual version of reorganizing your desk.

If you've known the answer for over a month and haven't moved, the obstacle isn't clarity. It's fear, or money, or a genuinely costly next step. Those are real problems. They're just not the one you keep researching.

---

## If you're staying, stay on purpose

Most of you aren't leaving tomorrow. Fine. The failure mode isn't staying — it's staying without *deciding to*, which is how eighteen months disappear.

Make it explicit:

- **A reassessment date in your calendar.** An actual date, not "after this project." Projects reproduce.
- **The conditions that would change your answer.** "If the reorg doesn't give me a different manager." Specific and falsifiable.
- **What you're extracting meanwhile.** Money, a title, a skill, a visa. Name it, so the cost has a purpose attached.
- **One exit-adjacent action per month,** even though you're not exiting. This is what keeps the stay a choice rather than a trap.

The difference between a strategic stay and slow attrition is entirely whether you wrote it down.

---

## What leaving fixes

Reliably: a bad manager, a toxic team, an untenable workload, an ethical conflict specific to that employer. Real things. If that's you, go.

Not reliably: over-functioning you'll reproduce anywhere, identity fused to output, dread of a discipline you've stopped believing in. Those travel, resurface around month six, and hit harder because you'd staked the recovery on the move.

So the question isn't "is this job bad." It's **what portion is the job, and what portion is how I do jobs?** Even a rough split changes your next move.

---

## 📬 Reader question

**"I recovered, went back to full capacity, and burned out again in fourteen months. What did I do wrong?"**

Probably nothing. You returned to the same system and it produced the same result on the same timeline. That's a machine working as designed.

One thing to examine: whether recovery was framed as *getting back to normal*. If normal broke you, restoring it is a return trip, not a repair. The people I know who avoided round three came back at 85% permanently and stopped treating the old baseline as the goal.

That's a real loss. It's also the trade that made the career survivable.
