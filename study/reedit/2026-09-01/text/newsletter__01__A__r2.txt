**THE SLOW BUILD**
*Issue #47*

---

**Subject:** Re-entry is the skill

---

Your routine didn't fail. Your re-entry did.

Nearly every reply to the September survey had the same shape: ran it five to seven weeks, got disrupted — travel, illness, one terrible work week — and never came back. The mechanics were fine. We covered those in the spring and they hold. The gap is what killed it.

Here's why. Practically, day eight is identical to day two: you get up, you do the thing. But two days off is a blip and eight days off is *evidence*. Coming back means admitting the evidence was real. Letting the whole thing quietly dissolve means never having that conversation. So people dissolve.

The fix is to make re-entry procedural — decided in advance, no self-assessment required.

---

## The protocol

Write this on an index card now, while nothing is broken. Not in Notion; you won't open Notion in a bad week.

> **If I've missed 3+ days:**
> Day one: floor version only.
> Day two: floor version only.
> Day three: resume normal.

Three rules govern it:

**Don't resume at full volume.** The instinct is to come back harder and make up ground. You get two good days and a second collapse, and the second one is worse — now it's a pattern instead of an incident.

**Don't evaluate during re-entry.** A lapse feels like information. It mostly isn't. Redesigning the routine after missing a week is avoidance in a productive costume: it feels like progress and requires no unpleasant mornings. Give it three normal days. If it still looks broken then, it might be.

**Don't treat the gap as debt.** There's nothing to make up. The chain metaphor is load-bearing for building and harmful for recovering — it prices a lapse as loss rather than pause.

---

## The other failure mode: drift

Routines also die by expansion. Mine went from twenty minutes to ninety over four months through entirely reasonable individual decisions, until I was doing preparation instead of work.

The tell isn't duration. It's that additions only ever happen on good days — and good days are when your judgment about sustainable volume is worst. You calibrate to a state you're in twice a week, miss it the other five, and read that as a discipline problem.

Quarterly audit, four questions:

- Actual duration, timed, not estimated?
- What would I genuinely miss if it vanished?
- What survives only because it's upstream of something I like?
- Is anything here load-bearing for the *day*, or just for the *routine*?

The last one does most of the work. Elements that exist to justify the routine's existence go first.

---

## When to kill it

Sometimes ending it is correct. Three signals: your life structure changed and the routine didn't; you can't start work without it (that's a dependency, not a support); or you've rebuilt it three times and it's failed the same way three times.

Killing it deliberately is cheap. Lapsing costs you the belief, which is the expensive part.

---

## This week

Write the re-entry card. Then time tomorrow's routine against what you'd have guessed. More than 30% off means drift — cut one element, and make it the one you'd defend hardest.
