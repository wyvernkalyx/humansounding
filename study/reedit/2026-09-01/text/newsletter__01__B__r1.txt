**THE SLOW BUILD**
*Issue #48 — Small changes, stacked over time*

---

**Subject:** You don't need another habit-stacking tutorial

**Preview:** For people on their fifth morning routine. The failure modes nobody writes about.

---

Last issue was the build. This one is for everyone who wrote back some version of: *I know all this. I've done all this. It still stops.*

Fair. If you've read the books, you already have the anchors and the two-minute rule and the never-miss-twice sign taped above your desk. Knowing more about habit formation is not your constraint. It hasn't been for years.

So let's skip the foundations and go at the things that actually break routines for people who already know how to build them.

---

## Failure mode 1: The floor became the ceiling

You shrank it until it was stupid. Good — that's the move, and it worked. One page. Three minutes. Shoes on, walk to the corner.

Then eighteen months later you're still doing one page.

The minimum viable version is a *ramp*, not a destination, but nothing in the system ever tells you to leave it. The whole apparatus is optimized against missing, and a floor you never exceed never triggers a miss. You have perfect adherence to something that stopped mattering.

**The fix:** hold two numbers, not one. A **floor** (never below, non-negotiable, protects the chain) and a **default** (what a normal day looks like when nothing has gone wrong). Floor: one page. Default: 700 words. Review the default quarterly; leave the floor alone forever.

If your default has been the same for a year, you're not maintaining a habit. You're maintaining a streak.

---

## Failure mode 2: You're defending a routine whose reason expired

The 6am writing block was built when you were finishing a book. The book came out in 2023. You are still getting up at 6 to write, and it now produces nothing you care about, and every morning has a faint smell of obligation you can't place.

This is the one I see most in people who are *good* at habits. The routine outlives the goal, gets absorbed into identity, and becomes very hard to kill precisely because you're proud of having kept it. Then it quietly consumes the hour that a different, currently-relevant thing needed.

**The fix:** expiration dates. Every element gets one — six or twelve months out. On that date it doesn't automatically continue; you have to re-argue for it. Most survive. The ones that don't were costing you an hour a day for reasons that stopped existing.

---

## Failure mode 3: You've never tested which part is load-bearing

Your morning has six elements. You believe it works. You don't know *what* works, because you've never run it without any given piece.

Almost certainly one or two elements are doing nearly all the work and the rest is ritual scaffolding that came along for the ride. That's not automatically bad — scaffolding has a real function, it makes the load-bearing part feel inevitable — but you should know which is which, because when life compresses your morning from 90 minutes to 20, you need to know what to cut without guessing.

**The fix:** subtractive testing. Remove one element for two weeks. Note what degrades — mood, output, sleep, how the afternoon goes. Restore, then remove the next. Takes a quarter to get through six items. You'll end up cutting at least one thing permanently, and you'll stop treating the sequence as a single indivisible object.

---

## Failure mode 4: You designed for your good weeks

You know the friction principles. But the routine you're running was still specified against a normal Tuesday — the 80% case — and normal Tuesdays are not where routines die. They die during the launch, the flu, the newborn, the move, the six weeks your parent was in hospital.

Anyone can hold a system at full capacity. The design question that actually matters is: **what does this look like at 20% capacity, and did I design that version deliberately, or am I improvising it while exhausted?**

**The fix:** write three tiers and name them, so switching is a decision rather than a collapse.

| Tier | When | What |
|---|---|---|
| Full | Normal weeks | The whole sequence, 75 min |
| Compressed | Travel, deadline, bad sleep | Load-bearing element only, 20 min |
| Survival | Crisis, illness, grief | Ten breaths outside. That's it. |

The point isn't the content. It's that dropping to Survival is a *move within the system* instead of evidence that the system failed. That distinction is worth more than any individual habit.

---

## The skill nobody trains: re-entry

Never-miss-twice handles a bad Tuesday. It does nothing for the real thing — the six-week gap, where you stopped and the reason you stopped was legitimate and now there's a wall of accumulated meaning around restarting.

Re-entry is a separate skill from building, and it's the one that determines whether you have a routine at 45 or a graveyard of nine-day attempts. Some things I've learned doing this badly, repeatedly:

**Restart smaller than feels dignified.** You'll want to resume at your old default. Don't — you'll fail in four days and add another layer to the wall. Restart at your floor, or below it. Your body doesn't remember your PR; it remembers six weeks of nothing.

**Don't diagnose first.** The urge is to figure out what went wrong before starting again. That's procrastination in a lab coat. Restart tiny for a week, *then* diagnose, when you have current data instead of a story.

**Kill the debt narrative.** There is no catch-up. Nothing is owed. The chain isn't real — it was a motivational fiction that was useful right up until the moment it started charging you interest.

**Change one surface thing.** New notebook, different room, different time. Enough novelty that today reads as a beginning instead of a failed continuation.

**Expect the first two weeks to feel worse than starting from zero.** They do. The comparison to your old competence is the whole cost, and it's temporary.

---

## A harder question than adherence

You are probably tracking whether you did the thing. That was the right metric in month one.

At this point the better question is: **is the downstream thing moving?**

A morning routine executed at 96% for two years that hasn't produced a manuscript, a change in bloodwork, a business, or a noticeably different internal weather is not a habit success. It's a very disciplined hobby. Adherence is a proxy, and like all proxies it becomes the target if you stare at it long enough.

So once a quarter, ignore the calendar of Xs entirely and ask: what is this hour actually *for*, and did it deliver? Sometimes the answer is "it's for feeling like myself," which is a completely legitimate output. But say it out loud, because if that's the goal, half your elements are probably optimized for the wrong thing.

---

## The renegotiation you're avoiding

Routines don't usually break. They get outgrown — by a baby, a promotion, a diagnosis, a relationship, a body that's ten years older than the one you designed for.

The failure isn't the change. It's spending eight months trying to force the old structure through new circumstances, treating each miss as a discipline problem, when the honest read is that the routine is now wrong and needs rebuilding from the constraints up.

Signals you're overdue:

- You've been at 60–70% adherence for two months and interpreting it as weakness
- You feel resentment, not resistance (different feelings — resistance is 6am grogginess, resentment is the sense that something is stealing from you)
- The routine only works on days that no longer occur regularly
- You're protecting it against people who have a real claim on that time

**The fix:** an annual teardown. Delete the whole thing on paper. Then rebuild from current constraints — actual wake time, actual obligations, actual energy, actual current goal — and see what you re-add. Usually 60% comes back unchanged, 20% comes back modified, 20% turns out to have been inertia. Do it in September rather than January, when you can think clearly and no one is watching.

No new habits. An audit:

1. List every element of your current morning, with its honest duration.
2. Mark each: **load-bearing**, **scaffolding**, or **unexamined**.
3. For each, write the goal it was originally built to serve — and whether that goal is still live.
4. Write your Compressed and Survival tiers, if you don't have them.
5. Pick the one unexamined element you're least willing to remove, and remove it for two weeks.
