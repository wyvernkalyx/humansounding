**THE SLOW BUILD**
*Issue #47 — Small changes, stacked over time*

---

**Subject:** The 5am routine that lasted nine days

**Preview:** Why your morning routine keeps collapsing, and the boring fix that works.

---

Two Januaries ago I built the perfect morning.

5:00 wake. Twenty minutes of meditation. Forty-five minutes of writing. Gym at 6:30. Cold shower. Journal. At my desk by 8:15 having already, as the internet promised, *won the day*.

It lasted nine days.

Day ten I slept through the alarm, skipped the whole thing, and felt so much like a failure that I didn't try again until March. That's the part people don't mention: a routine that collapses doesn't just cost you the routine. It costs you the belief that you're the kind of person who can keep one.

So this issue is about the unglamorous version — the one that survives a bad night's sleep, a sick kid, a 6am flight, and February.

---

## Why morning routines fail

Almost always one of these four:

**1. You designed it for a fantasy version of you.**
The routine you sketch on a Sunday night is written by your most optimistic self, and executed by your 6am self, who is a completely different person with completely different opinions. Design for the 6am guy. He's grumpy and he's in charge.

**2. It's all-or-nothing.**
A seven-part routine has seven points of failure. Miss the workout and the whole sequence feels broken, so you skip the rest. Fragility scales with complexity.

**3. There's no anchor.**
"Meditate in the morning" isn't a plan, it's a hope. Habits need to attach to something that already reliably happens.

**4. It actually starts the night before.**
You cannot out-discipline a 1am bedtime. Most "morning routine problems" are evening problems wearing a disguise.

## The build

### Step 1: Pick one thing. One.

Not a routine — a keystone. Ask: *if I only got to protect one thing every morning for the next year, which one would change the most?*

Common answers: movement, writing, reading, sitting quietly, a real breakfast, ten minutes of planning before the inbox opens.

You'll add more later. But a one-item routine has a 100% completion rate on days you do it, and completion is what builds identity. Six items means you go 4-for-6 and feel like you lost.

### Step 2: Shrink it until it feels stupid

Whatever you picked, cut it down until skipping it would be more effort than doing it.

- Exercise → put on running shoes and walk to the corner
- Writing → one paragraph
- Meditation → three minutes
- Reading → one page

Yes, it's too small to produce results. That's fine — you're not optimizing for results yet, you're optimizing for *never missing*. Results come from the version of this that still exists in month eight.

The rule I use: **the floor is tiny, the ceiling is open.** You must do one page. You may do forty. Most mornings you'll do more, but the obligation stays small, which is exactly why it survives the bad days.

### Step 3: Anchor it to something already automatic

Format: **After I [existing thing], I will [new thing] in [place].**

- After I pour my coffee, I'll write one paragraph at the kitchen table.
- After I brush my teeth, I'll do three minutes of stretching on the bedroom floor.

Pick an anchor that happens *every* day, not most days. "After my commute" dies the moment you work from home. "After I put the kettle on" is bulletproof.

Then say it out loud, or write it on a sticky note and put it on the anchor object. Specificity is doing most of the work here — research on implementation intentions consistently shows that naming *when* and *where* roughly doubles follow-through compared to intention alone.

### Step 4: Move the friction to the night before

This is the highest-leverage step and it takes four minutes.

Your morning self has almost no capacity for decisions or setup. Your 9pm self has plenty. So use 9pm you as staff:

- Clothes out (workout gear where you'll trip on it)
- Document open on the screen, cursor in place
- Coffee loaded, mug on the counter
- Phone charging **in another room**
- One sentence on paper: the first thing you'll do tomorrow

The phone one is not optional in my experience. If the first input of your day is other people's demands, the routine is over before it starts — you're now reacting, and reacting mode is very hard to exit.

### Step 5: Write the bad-morning version *now*

Every routine needs a documented minimum. Decide it today, while you're calm, so you're not negotiating with yourself at 6:40 on four hours of sleep.

> Full: 30 min walk + journal + slow breakfast
> **Bad morning: step outside and take ten breaths.**

The bad-morning version isn't a consolation prize. It's the whole point. It keeps the chain intact and keeps you the kind of person who does this, which is the actual asset.

### Step 6: Never miss twice

You will miss. Missing once is noise. Missing twice is how a routine becomes a former routine.

So drop the streak-counting — streaks make one bad day feel catastrophic. Instead put an X on a wall calendar and hold one rule: **two blanks in a row is the emergency.** That's the only metric that matters.

## The ramp

Roughly how I'd stage it:

| Weeks | What you're doing |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | One tiny habit, anchored. Nothing else changes. |
| 3–4 | Same habit, let the duration grow naturally. Add the night-before setup. |
| 5–6 | Add a second element — only if the first one now feels automatic. |
| 7+ | Adjust wake time if (and only if) bedtime has already moved. |

Notice wake time is last. Everyone starts there. It's the hardest variable and the least important one — a consistent 7:00 beats an inconsistent 5:30 every single time. If you want to wake earlier, move your bedtime first and let the mornings follow.

---

## Five traps worth naming

- **Copying someone else's routine.** That founder's 4:30am block works because they have no children and a home gym. Steal the structure, not the schedule.
- **Optimizing before you're consistent.** Don't research the best journaling method. Write badly for two weeks first.
- **Routine as procrastination.** If your morning routine has grown to 2.5 hours and you still haven't started the actual work, the routine has become the avoidance.
- **Requiring perfect conditions.** Travel, illness, and chaos are not exceptions to normal life — they *are* normal life, about 20% of the time. Your routine has to work at hotel-room quality or it doesn't work.
- **Judging it by feel.** Some mornings it feels transcendent, most it feels like nothing. Neither is data. Ninety days is data.

## This week's experiment

Do exactly this, and nothing more:

1. Name one keystone habit.
2. Shrink it to a version that takes under five minutes.
3. Write your anchor sentence: *After I ______, I will ______ at/in ______.*
4. Write your bad-morning version underneath it.
5. Tonight, do the four-minute setup.

Run it for seven days. Don't add anything, even if you feel great on day three — *especially* if you feel great on day three. That enthusiasm is precisely what talked you into the nine-day version.

## From the mailbag

**Q: I work nights / rotating shifts. Is a morning routine even possible?**

Yes, but stop calling it morning. What you actually want is a *first-hour routine* — a consistent sequence attached to waking, whenever that is. The anchor approach works better for you than most people, because "after I make my first coffee" travels across any schedule while "at 6:30am" doesn't. Keep the sequence identical, let the clock time float.
