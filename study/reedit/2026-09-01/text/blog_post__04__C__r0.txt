# What to Look for When Buying a Used Car

Buying a used car is one of those tasks that feels like it should be simple and almost never is. You're trying to evaluate a complex machine, usually in a parking lot, usually in under an hour, usually while someone is standing next to you explaining why three other people are interested in it.

The good news: you don't need to be a mechanic to avoid the worst mistakes. You mostly need a process, a bit of patience, and the willingness to walk away. Here's how to do it.

## Start with the total cost, not the sticker price

Before you look at a single listing, figure out what you can actually afford — and remember that the purchase price is only part of it.

Factor in sales tax and registration fees (often several hundred to a few thousand dollars), insurance (get a quote *before* you buy — rates vary wildly by model, and that sporty coupe may cost more to insure than you'd think), and a realistic maintenance budget. A good rule of thumb is to set aside $1,000–$1,500 for the first year of ownership on any used car. Something will need doing: tires, brakes, a battery, a timing belt.

Also research the specific model's reliability and common failure points. Every car has a reputation, and a fifteen-minute search for "common problems [year, make, model]" on owner forums will tell you more than any brochure. If a particular model is known for transmission failures at 90,000 miles, you want to know that before you're looking at one with 87,000.

## Where you buy changes what you're getting

**Franchise dealers** (the ones with a brand name on the building) charge the most but often offer certified pre-owned programs with real warranties and inspection standards. If you want the lowest-hassle option and don't mind paying for it, this is it.

**Independent used lots** vary enormously. Some are excellent. Some are moving auction cars with hidden histories. Reviews and reputation matter a lot here.

**Private sellers** typically offer the best prices because there's no overhead, but you get no warranty and no recourse. Everything is as-is. This is where a careful inspection matters most — and where you can learn the most, because a private seller can tell you how the car has actually been driven and maintained.

Wherever you buy, be wary of "curbstoners" — unlicensed dealers posing as private sellers to dodge regulations. Red flags include a seller whose name isn't on the title, meeting in a random parking lot rather than a home, and having several other cars for sale.

## Get the history report — and read it properly

Run the VIN through a service like Carfax or AutoCheck, or check the free NMVTIS-based reports. You're looking for:

- **Title brands**: salvage, rebuilt, flood, lemon, or odometer discrepancy. These substantially reduce value and can make insurance and resale difficult. Not automatically disqualifying, but you need to know.
- **Accident history**: minor fender-benders are common and often fine. Frame or structural damage is a different conversation.
- **Number of owners and geography**: a car that spent ten winters in a road-salt state will have more rust than the same car from Arizona.
- **Service records**: consistent maintenance is a genuinely good sign.

One important caveat: history reports are incomplete. Accidents repaired without insurance claims don't show up. A clean report is reassuring, not proof.

## The walkaround: what to actually look at

Inspect the car in daylight, when it's dry, and when the engine is cold. A seller who insists on meeting at night or has already warmed the car up may be hiding something — cold starts reveal problems that a warm engine masks.

**Body and paint.** Crouch down and sight along the side of the car. Ripples, waves, or paint that doesn't quite match between panels suggest bodywork. Check the gaps between doors, hood, and fenders — they should be even and consistent on both sides. Look for overspray on rubber trim and inside door jambs. Open the hood and trunk and look at the bolts holding fenders and hinges: tool marks on the paint mean something was removed and replaced.

**Rust.** Surface rust on the underside is normal on older cars. What you don't want is bubbling paint at the bottom of doors and wheel arches, or flaky, layered rust on frame rails and suspension mounting points. Bring a flashlight and actually look underneath.

**Tires.** Four matching tires with even wear is a good sign. Uneven wear on the inside or outside edges suggests alignment or suspension issues. Mismatched brands or wildly different tread depths tell you the owner was cutting corners — which they probably did elsewhere too. Check the four-digit date code on the sidewall; tires older than six years should be replaced regardless of tread.

**Fluids.** Engine oil should be brown to black, not milky or gritty. Coolant should be brightly colored, not rusty or oily. Transmission fluid (if there's a dipstick) should be reddish and shouldn't smell burnt. Look for crusty residue or wet spots around gaskets and hoses.

**Interior.** Wear should roughly match the mileage — a 40,000-mile car with a shiny, worn steering wheel and sagging driver's seat is suspicious. Pull up a corner of the carpet in the footwells and check for dampness, silt, or a musty smell, all signs of water intrusion. Test every button: windows, locks, mirrors, seat adjusters, climate control (both heat and A/C), infotainment, and every light.

## The test drive

Drive for at least twenty minutes and include a mix of city streets and highway speeds. Turn the radio off.

Pay attention to:

- **Cold start**: any knocking, ticking, or blue/white smoke from the exhaust
- **Braking**: pulling to one side, pulsation through the pedal, grinding, or a soft/spongy pedal
- **Steering**: pulling, vibration at highway speed, excessive play, clunking over bumps
- **Transmission**: automatic shifts should be smooth and prompt without flaring or hard jolts; manual clutches shouldn't slip or grab right at the top of travel
- **Warning lights**: any light on the dash needs an explanation. A seller who says "that's just the sensor" is telling you they never fixed it.

Find an empty lot and turn the wheel to full lock in both directions while moving slowly — clicking or grinding suggests CV joint or steering issues.

## Get a pre-purchase inspection

This is the single most valuable thing you can do, and the step people skip most often.

For $100–$200, an independent mechanic will put the car on a lift and tell you what you can't see: frame damage, leaks, worn suspension components, brake and tire life remaining, and upcoming maintenance. Even on a car that checks out, the report gives you negotiating leverage.

**If a seller refuses to allow an inspection, walk away.** There is no good reason to say no. This is not negotiable, and it applies to dealers too.

## The paperwork

Confirm the VIN on the dashboard matches the VIN on the title, the door jamb sticker, and the history report. Make sure the seller's name is on the title and that there are no liens listed — if there's an outstanding loan, the lender holds the title, and you'll need to handle payoff carefully, ideally at the lender's bank.

At a dealership, ask for the **out-the-door price** in writing, itemized. This is where dealer-added fees appear: documentation fees, "market adjustments," paint protection, VIN etching, and warranty products with big margins. Most of these are negotiable or refusable. Negotiate the total price, not the monthly payment — a lower monthly payment stretched over 84 months is not a discount.

If you're financing, get pre-approved by your own bank or credit union first. It gives you a rate to beat and removes a major lever from the dealer's side of the table.

## The most important skill

Be willing to leave.

Every pressure tactic in the used car world — the other interested buyer, the price that's only good today, the deposit to "hold it" — works only on people who feel they've already committed. You haven't. There are millions of used cars, and another one that fits your criteria will appear next week.

The car that's right for you will still be right for you after you've slept on it, gotten it inspected, and checked the numbers twice. If a seller can't accommodate that, they've told you something useful.

Happy hunting.
