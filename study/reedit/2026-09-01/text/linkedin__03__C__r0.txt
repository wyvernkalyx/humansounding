**Eighteen months ago I pitched [project]. Last [month], we shut it down.**

We were solving a problem that wasn't a problem.

Not for lack of effort. The team shipped on time, the tech worked, the demo was genuinely impressive. But we'd built the whole thing on an assumption we never seriously tested: that [customers would pay to solve X]. When we finally put it in front of real users, they were polite. Politeness is the worst signal you can get.

Three things I took away:

**Enthusiasm isn't validation.** Early conversations were full of "that's interesting" and "keep me posted." I read those as demand. They were manners.

**The expensive mistakes happen before you write any code.** We spent [X months] building and [two weeks] on discovery. That ratio should have been closer to reversed.

**Killing it sooner would have been the braver call.** I saw the warning signs around month [six] and talked myself out of them, because I'd already sold the vision internally and didn't want to be the person who changed their mind. Sunk cost is an ego problem as much as a finance one.

The project failed. I'd argue the team didn't — we know more about [our market] now than any competitor deck could have taught us.
