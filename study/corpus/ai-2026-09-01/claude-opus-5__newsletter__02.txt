**Issue #47 — "The Decade Nobody Prepares You For"**

---

**Subject line:** Your twenties are a math problem (and you're winning by default)

---

Hey friends,

Someone emailed me last week with a line I can't stop thinking about:

> *"I make $58k, I have no idea where it goes, and every article I read assumes I already have $10,000 sitting around to invest."*

Yeah. That's the gap. Most personal finance content is written either for people in crisis or people with a paid-off house and a boat problem. Very little of it is written for the specific chaos of being 24 with a Roth IRA you opened once and never funded.

So this issue is that. No shame, no "skip the latte," no assuming you have a spare $10k. Just the order of operations.

---

## 🧭 THE MAIN THING: Stop optimizing, start sequencing

The number one mistake I see in this decade isn't overspending. It's **doing the right things in the wrong order** — paying extra on student loans while carrying a 24% credit card balance, or aggressively investing with $0 in savings and then selling those investments at a loss when the car dies.

Money in your twenties is less about discipline and more about sequence. Here's the order that works for almost everyone:

**1. Get to a $1,000–2,000 cash buffer.**
Not a full emergency fund. Just enough that a flat tire is an inconvenience, not a credit card balance. This is the single highest-ROI move you'll make, and it takes most people 2–4 months.

**2. Take the full employer 401(k) match.**
If your job matches 4% and you contribute 3%, you are declining part of your salary. There is no investment on earth with a guaranteed 50–100% instant return. Do this even if you have debt.

**3. Kill anything above ~7% interest.**
Credit cards, payday loans, some private student loans. Paying off a 22% APR card is a guaranteed 22% return. The stock market has never guaranteed you anything.

**4. Build the real emergency fund: 3–6 months of essential expenses.**
"Essential" means rent, food, insurance, minimums, transport — not your current lifestyle. Keep it in a high-yield savings account, not checking, not stocks. Right now HYSAs are paying meaningfully more than the ~0.01% big banks offer. Moving your savings is a 15-minute task that can be worth several hundred dollars a year.

**5. Now invest for real.**
Max a Roth IRA if you're eligible (2025 limit: $7,000/year — check current limits, they move). Then go back and increase your 401(k). A low-cost total market or target-date index fund is a completely legitimate answer here. You do not need to pick stocks. You will probably do worse if you try.

**6. Everything else: your call.**
Extra debt payoff, a house fund, a sabbatical fund, a "I want to quit this job in 18 months" fund. This is where personality enters the chat.

**If you only remember one line:** *Buffer → Match → High-interest debt → Emergency fund → Invest.*

## 📊 THE NUMBER THIS WEEK: $280,000

That's roughly what a 10-year delay costs you.

Two people invest $200/month at a 7% average annual return.

- **Alex starts at 25.** By 65: about **$525,000**. Total contributed: $96,000.
- **Sam starts at 35.** By 65: about **$244,000**. Total contributed: $72,000.

Sam "saved" $24,000 by waiting — and gave up about $280,000.

This is why "I'll get serious about money when I earn more" is the most expensive sentence in your twenties. The variable that matters most right now isn't the amount. It's the start date.

*(7% is an illustrative long-run average, not a promise. Returns are lumpy, ugly, and non-linear in real life.)*

## 🔥 MYTH WE'RE BURNING: "Budgeting means tracking every dollar"

It doesn't, and that's exactly why most budgets die by week three.

Try **reverse budgeting** instead. You automate the things that matter and spend the rest freely without logging a single coffee:

1. Paycheck lands.
2. Automatic transfers fire the same day: retirement, savings, extra debt payment.
3. Bills auto-pay from a dedicated account.
4. Whatever's left in checking is *yours*. Spend it on nonsense. Guilt-free.

You've made one decision instead of four hundred. Behavioral research is very consistent on this: automation beats willpower, every single time. Your future self is not a more disciplined person. Your future self is you, but tired.

## 💬 READER QUESTION

> **"I got a $9k raise. Everyone says don't inflate my lifestyle, but I've been eating rice and stress for three years. Am I allowed to enjoy it?"** — M., Denver

Yes. Please. Here's the split I'd suggest: **save/invest 50%, spend 50%.**

Total lifestyle deflation isn't a virtue, it's a slow leak in your motivation. But if 100% of every raise gets absorbed into a nicer apartment and better takeout, you'll be 35 earning double and saving the same nothing.

Fifty percent of that raise is about $290/month invested — which, if you're 26, is genuinely life-altering over 40 years. The other half buys you a life you don't need to escape from. Both things are the point.

## ✅ ONE THING TO DO THIS WEEK

**Log into your 401(k) and find two numbers:** your contribution percentage, and your employer's match formula.

That's it. Not a spreadsheet. Not a budget overhaul. Two numbers.

An enormous number of people are contributing below their match and don't know it. Fifteen minutes, potentially thousands of dollars a year. Best hourly rate you'll get all month.

## 🔗 QUICK HITS

- **Check your credit report free** at annualcreditreport.com (the actual government-authorized site). Errors are more common than people think, and they're fixable.
- **Raise your deductible, lower your premium** — but only if your emergency fund can absorb the deductible. Otherwise you're just moving risk around.
- **The 24-hour rule** for any purchase over $100. Cart it, sleep on it. You'll buy maybe a third of what you would have.
- **Talk about money with a friend this month.** Salaries, rent, debt, whatever you can stomach. Financial secrecy overwhelmingly benefits employers and lenders, not you.

---

## 🎧 IF YOU HAVE 20 MINUTES

Go pull up your last 30 days of transactions and just... look. Don't categorize, don't judge, don't build a system. Just read them like a story about a stranger.

Most people find one or two subscriptions they forgot existed and one spending pattern they didn't know was a pattern. That's usually enough to change something. Awareness does a shocking amount of work on its own.

---

That's the issue. Your twenties are financially messy for structural reasons — entry-level wages, high rent, student debt, and an economy that didn't ask your permission. Not because you're bad with money.

But you're also holding the one asset that no amount of future income can buy back: **time.** Use a little of it this week.
