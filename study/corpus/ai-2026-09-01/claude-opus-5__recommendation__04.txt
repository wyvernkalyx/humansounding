## Performance Review — [Name], Senior Software Engineer
**Review period:** [Dates] · **Reviewer:** [Manager] · **Overall rating:** Meets expectations *(strong technical performance offset by collaboration gaps)*

### Summary

[Name] is one of the strongest technical contributors on the team. The [payments migration] would not have shipped on schedule without their work, and their instincts on system design have repeatedly saved us from expensive mistakes. That contribution is real and valued.

At the same time, the way [Name] works with others is currently limiting their impact and, in several cases, has had a measurable cost to team velocity and morale. At the senior level, technical output and the ability to make others more effective are not separate things — the second is part of the job description. This review treats it that way.

### Strengths

**Technical depth and judgment.** [Name] led the design of [the event-driven order pipeline] and correctly identified the consistency issue in the original proposal that would have surfaced in production under load. Their [database indexing work in Q3] cut p99 latency on the [checkout] endpoint by [40%].

**Ownership under pressure.** During the [March incident], [Name] took point on diagnosis, worked the problem for [six hours], and wrote a root-cause analysis that was genuinely excellent — clear, blameless, and actionable. Two of the follow-ups have already prevented repeat incidents.

**Code quality.** Their code is well-tested, well-structured, and rarely comes back. Reviewers trust it. That's a real asset.

### Areas for development

**Code review tone.** Multiple engineers have raised that [Name]'s review comments read as dismissive. Specific examples: "[this is obviously wrong]" on [Engineer A]'s PR in [January]; "[did you even read the RFC]" on [Engineer B]'s in [February]. The technical points in both cases were correct. The framing meant they landed as criticism of the person rather than the code. Two engineers have told me they now route PRs around [Name] when they can, which means we lose the benefit of their review entirely — the opposite of what anyone wants.

**Disagreement in group settings.** In the [architecture review on March 12] and again in [the Q2 planning session], [Name] continued to relitigate a decision after the group had converged, in a way that visibly shut down further discussion. Being right isn't sufficient — a decision the team doesn't feel ownership of gets implemented badly. When [Name] does disagree and commit, they do it well; the issue is how long it takes to get there and what that period costs the room.

**Knowledge concentration.** [Name] is the only person who understands [the reconciliation service] end to end. Requests to document it or pair on it have been deprioritized repeatedly in favor of new feature work. This is a business risk and it also caps [Name]'s own growth — it's hard to promote someone who can't be moved off their current system.

**Responsiveness to junior engineers.** [Engineer C] has reported that questions to [Name] often go unanswered for [days] or get one-line replies that don't unblock them. [Name] has more context here than anyone; withholding it, even unintentionally, is expensive.

### Goals for next period

1. **Code review:** Adopt a comment convention that separates blocking from non-blocking feedback and states the *why* behind each request. Target: zero escalations to me about review tone this period; positive shift in peer feedback at midyear.
2. **Meetings:** In design discussions, state objections once with reasoning, then explicitly say whether you're blocking or disagreeing-and-committing. I'll give you a private signal if I see the pattern recurring.
3. **Knowledge transfer:** Ship an architecture doc for [the reconciliation service] by [date] and pair with [Engineer C] on the next two substantial changes to it. This is a hard deliverable, not a stretch goal.
4. **Mentorship:** Own onboarding for the next engineer joining the team. Weekly 1:1 with them for their first eight weeks.

### Manager's note

I want to be direct about stakes, because vagueness here wouldn't be fair to you. [Name]'s technical ceiling is as high as anyone's on this team. Promotion to [Staff] is realistic within [12–18 months] on technical merit alone. It will not happen without visible movement on the items above, because a staff engineer's leverage comes almost entirely through other people. Right now the trajectory is flat on that axis.

I also want to name something [Name] has raised with me: the frustration of watching decisions get made slowly or watching code ship that they'd have written better. That frustration is legitimate. The goal here isn't for [Name] to stop caring or to soften their standards. It's to spend the credibility they've earned more deliberately, so that high standards read as investment in the team rather than judgment of it.

I'm committed to supporting this — biweekly 1:1s focused on these goals, and I'll surface specific incidents in real time rather than saving them for the next review cycle.

**Two notes on delivering this:**

- **No surprises.** If any of the above is the first time [Name] is hearing it, the review will read as an ambush and they'll discount all of it. Raise each item verbally at least once before it appears in writing.
- **Don't hedge the rating.** If you write "meets expectations" but the conversation is about a performance problem, the written record and the verbal message diverge and the employee will believe the more comfortable one. Rate what you actually mean.

Happy to adjust tone — I can make this sharper if the situation is closer to a formal warning, or warmer if this is a first-time nudge with a strong performer you're worried about losing.
