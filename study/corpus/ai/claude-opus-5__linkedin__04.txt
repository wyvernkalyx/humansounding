**The best hire I ever made was, on paper, the least qualified candidate in the process.**

She had none of the industry experience we'd listed as "required." Her portfolio was thin. She'd come from an adjacent field entirely, and half my panel wanted to pass.

Eighteen months later, she was outperforming people with a decade on her.

I've thought a lot about why — and I've come to believe most of us are optimizing for the wrong thing.

**Experience tells you what someone has already done. Attitude tells you what they'll do next.**

Here's the uncomfortable truth: experience is the easiest thing to verify and the least predictive of success in a role that's changing. Tools change. Processes change. Entire job functions get reinvented every few years. What someone knew in 2021 is depreciating faster than we like to admit.

What doesn't depreciate:

→ **Curiosity.** The instinct to ask "why does it work this way?" before assuming it's broken — or fine.

→ **Coachability.** The ability to receive hard feedback without treating it as an attack on their identity.

→ **Ownership.** The difference between "that wasn't my responsibility" and "I noticed it, so I flagged it."

→ **Resilience.** Not toughness for its own sake. Just the capacity to be wrong, recalibrate, and keep going.

→ **Generosity.** People who make the team around them better. You feel their absence more than their presence.

None of these show up on a CV. All of them compound.

**But let me be clear about what I'm *not* saying.**

"Hire for attitude" is not a licence to hire your friends, or people who interview charmingly, or people who remind you of yourself. That's not attitude — that's affinity bias wearing a nicer jacket.

It's also not an argument for hiring the unqualified into roles where competence is non-negotiable. I want my surgeon to have done this before. Some roles genuinely require deep, hard-won expertise, and pretending otherwise is reckless.

The real question isn't *attitude or experience*. It's: **which parts of this role can we teach, and which parts can't we?**

Software, workflows, domain knowledge, our particular way of doing things — teachable. Usually within six months.

Intellectual honesty, work ethic, how someone behaves when a project goes sideways at 5pm on a Friday — not teachable. Certainly not by me.

**So how do you actually assess for it?**

Stop asking hypotheticals. "How would you handle conflict?" tells you nothing except how well someone performs interviews.

Ask instead:
- "Tell me about a time you were clearly wrong about something important. What happened?"
- "What's a piece of feedback that stung — and what did you do with it?"
- "What have you taught yourself in the last year, and why?"

Then listen for specificity. People describing real experiences give you texture, dates, names, the messy bits. People performing give you polished abstractions.

And notice who takes accountability versus who narrates a story where they're the only reasonable person in it.

The candidate you almost passed on might be the one who reshapes your team.
