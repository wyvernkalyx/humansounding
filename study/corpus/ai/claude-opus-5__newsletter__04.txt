**THE MIDWEEK MARGIN**
*Issue #47 — Burnout Isn't a Willpower Problem*

---

Hi there,

A reader wrote in last week with a line that stuck with me: "I don't need a vacation. I came back from one three weeks ago and felt worse."

That sentence is the clearest description of burnout I've heard in a while. Because burnout isn't tiredness. Tiredness responds to rest. Burnout doesn't — which is why so many people conclude something must be wrong with *them*.

It usually isn't.

**What burnout actually is**

Researcher Christina Maslach, who spent decades studying this, describes burnout as having three parts: exhaustion, cynicism, and a shrinking sense of your own effectiveness. That middle one is the tell. When you start feeling detached from work you used to care about — or quietly contemptuous of it — that's not a character flaw. That's a protective mechanism kicking in.

Her other finding matters more: burnout is a mismatch between a person and their job, not a deficiency in the person. Six mismatches show up again and again:

- **Workload** — more than can be sustainably done
- **Control** — little say over how you do it
- **Reward** — effort that goes unacknowledged
- **Community** — isolation or unresolved conflict
- **Fairness** — decisions that feel arbitrary
- **Values** — being asked to do things you don't believe in

Notice how few of those are fixed by a spa day.

**A more useful first question**

Instead of "how do I recover?" try: **which of those six is loudest for me right now?**

Most people assume it's workload. Often it isn't. Plenty of us have worked brutal hours happily, on a team we trusted, doing something that mattered. Take that same workload and add a manager who moves the goalposts, and it becomes unbearable within a month.

Naming the actual mismatch changes what you do next. Fairness problems need conversations or exits. Control problems sometimes need a renegotiated scope. Values problems rarely get better by trying harder.

**Three things that genuinely help**

**1. Reduce something real.** Not "be more efficient" — actually remove a commitment, a meeting, a project. Efficiency gains get absorbed by new work. Removals hold.

**2. Rebuild one relationship at work.** The single strongest buffer against burnout in the research isn't rest; it's social support. One colleague you can be honest with does more than a productivity system.

**3. Protect a small amount of work you find meaningful.** Even 10% of your week spent on something you'd choose voluntarily changes how the other 90% lands. Guard it like a meeting with your CEO.

**And one thing to stop doing**

Stop treating recovery as a project to optimize. Turning rest into another performance — the perfect sleep routine, the tracked meditation streak — just moves the pressure somewhere new.

**If you're past the point of small adjustments**

Sometimes the honest answer is that the job is the problem and no amount of boundary-setting will fix it. That's worth knowing sooner rather than later. Burnout that goes unaddressed for years doesn't stay at work — it follows you home, and recovery gets longer the further in you go. A doctor or therapist is a reasonable next step, not an overreaction.

You're not broken. The fit is off.
