# What to Look For When Buying a Used Car

Buying a used car can save you thousands of dollars — the steepest depreciation happens in the first two or three years of a vehicle's life, and someone else has already absorbed that hit. But used cars come with unknowns. Here's how to shop smart.

## Start With Homework, Not a Test Drive

Before you look at a single listing, figure out your total budget — not just the purchase price, but insurance, registration, and expected maintenance. Then narrow down to two or three models that fit your needs and have solid reliability records. Owner forums are goldmines here: search "[model] [year] common problems" and you'll quickly learn whether a car has a known transmission issue or an oil-burning engine at 90,000 miles.

## Vehicle History Is Non-Negotiable

Get the VIN and run a history report through Carfax or AutoCheck. You're looking for accident records, salvage or flood titles, odometer inconsistencies, and how many owners the car has had. A clean report isn't a guarantee — not every accident gets reported — but a bad one is a clear signal to walk away.

Also check whether any open recalls exist. In the U.S., the NHTSA website lets you look this up free by VIN.

## Inspect It Yourself in Daylight

Never evaluate a car at night or in the rain; both hide flaws beautifully. When you see it in person, look for:

- **Mismatched paint or uneven panel gaps**, which suggest bodywork after a collision
- **Rust**, especially along wheel wells, rocker panels, and the underside
- **Tire wear** — uneven wear points to alignment or suspension problems, and four mismatched tires suggest deferred maintenance
- **Fluids** — oil should be brown, not milky or gritty; transmission fluid should be reddish, not dark and burnt
- **Interior wear** that doesn't match the odometer reading, like a worn-through driver's seat on a "45,000-mile" car

Check that every electrical component works: windows, locks, A/C, heat, infotainment, lights.

## The Test Drive

Drive it for at least 20 minutes on varied roads, including highway speeds. Turn the radio off and listen. Do you hear clunks over bumps, whining from the transmission, or grinding when braking? Does the car pull to one side? Does the steering wheel shake at 60 mph? Test hard braking in a safe, empty area — the car should stop straight without pulsing.

Start the engine cold if possible. Many problems only show up in the first minute of a cold start.

## Pay for a Pre-Purchase Inspection

This is the single best money you'll spend. For $100–$200, an independent mechanic will put the car on a lift and tell you what you're actually buying. If a seller refuses to allow one, that's your answer — walk away.

## Handle the Paperwork Carefully

Make sure the seller's name matches the title and that there are no liens listed. Be wary of "curbstoners" — unlicensed dealers posing as private sellers, often flipping problem cars. If someone is selling several vehicles or won't meet at their home, be skeptical.

## Finally: Be Willing to Walk

The most valuable thing in any negotiation is your willingness to leave. There is always another car. Pressure, urgency, and "I have another buyer coming in an hour" are sales tactics, not facts. Take your time, and you'll end up with a car you're happy with for years.
