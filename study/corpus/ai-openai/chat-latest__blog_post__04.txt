# What to Look for When Buying a Used Car

Buying a used car can save you thousands of dollars, but a low price doesn’t necessarily mean a good deal. A vehicle that looks spotless in an online listing could be hiding accident damage, neglected maintenance, or expensive mechanical problems.

Whether you’re shopping at a dealership or buying from a private seller, here’s what to check before handing over your money.

## Start With the Vehicle’s History

Before you get too attached to a car, look into its history. Use the vehicle identification number (VIN) to obtain a history report from a reputable service. Depending on where you live, the report may reveal previous accidents, title problems, odometer discrepancies, ownership history, and other useful information.

A history report is a helpful screening tool, but it isn’t a substitute for an inspection. Not every accident or repair gets reported.

Ask the seller for maintenance records as well. Receipts showing regular oil changes and scheduled services can tell you a lot about how the vehicle has been treated. Pay particular attention to major maintenance items specified by the manufacturer, such as timing-belt replacement.

## Inspect the Exterior Carefully

Look at the car in daylight and, if possible, when it’s dry. Walk around it slowly and check for dents, rust, scratches, cracked lights, and damaged trim.

Look at the gaps between body panels. Uneven gaps, mismatched paint, overspray, or panels with noticeably different shades can indicate previous bodywork. Repairs aren’t automatically a reason to reject a car, but you should know what was repaired and why.

Check the tires, too. All four should ideally be appropriate for the vehicle and have adequate tread. Uneven wear can point to alignment, suspension, or tire-maintenance issues.

## Check the Interior

Wear and tear should make sense for the car’s age and claimed mileage. Heavily worn pedals, seats, or steering-wheel surfaces on a supposedly low-mileage car deserve a closer look.

Test everything you reasonably can: windows, locks, mirrors, air conditioning, heating, infotainment system, cameras, lights, seat adjustments, and any driver-assistance features.

Watch for water damage as well. Damp carpets, stains, corrosion, or persistent musty odors can be warning signs of leaks or previous flooding.

## Look Under the Hood

You don’t need to be a mechanic to spot obvious problems. Check for visible fluid leaks, damaged wiring, severe corrosion, or components that appear poorly repaired.

With the engine cool and the car on level ground, inspect accessible fluid levels according to the owner’s manual. Very low fluids or fluids in obviously poor condition may suggest neglected maintenance, although appearance alone doesn't diagnose a problem.

Ask the seller not to warm up the vehicle before you arrive. Starting a cold engine can reveal problems that are less noticeable once the engine is warm.

## Pay Attention During the Test Drive

Never buy a used car based solely on how it looks. A proper test drive should include different speeds and, where practical and legal, city streets and highway driving.

Pay attention to:

- Difficulty starting, rough idling, smoke, or unusual engine noises
- Warning lights that remain illuminated
- Hesitation, slipping, jerking, or unusual noises when accelerating or shifting
- Vibration or pulling while driving
- Grinding, squealing, pulsation, or pulling while braking
- Clunks, rattles, or excessive bouncing over uneven roads
- Steering that feels unusually loose, heavy, or inconsistent

Turn the radio off for part of the drive so you can hear what the car is doing.

## Get an Independent Pre-Purchase Inspection

This is one of the most valuable steps in buying a used vehicle. Before completing the purchase, have the car inspected by an independent mechanic you trust.

A professional can identify problems you might miss, including worn suspension parts, leaks, structural or collision damage, brake wear, diagnostic trouble codes, and upcoming maintenance needs. The inspection may cost some money upfront, but it can prevent a much larger repair bill later.

If a seller refuses to allow a reasonable independent inspection, consider that a significant warning sign.

## Research the Model, Not Just the Individual Car

Even a well-maintained example can have weaknesses common to its model or powertrain. Research the specific model year, engine, transmission, recalls, known reliability issues, typical repair costs, fuel economy, and insurance rates.

Also check whether any safety recalls remain unresolved. In many countries, government or manufacturer websites let you search by VIN.

## Calculate the Real Price

The advertised price isn't necessarily what the vehicle will ultimately cost you. Account for taxes, registration, documentation or dealer fees, insurance, financing costs, and repairs or maintenance the car needs immediately.

Compare the asking price with similar vehicles of the same model year, trim, mileage, condition, and location. A suspiciously cheap vehicle deserves extra scrutiny rather than automatic excitement.

If you’re financing the purchase, compare the total amount you’ll repay—not merely the monthly payment.

## Verify the Paperwork

Before paying, make sure the VIN on the vehicle matches the VIN on the title or registration documents. Confirm that the person or business selling the vehicle has the legal right to sell it and investigate any outstanding liens according to your local rules.

Read the sales agreement carefully before signing. Warranty rights and disclosure requirements vary considerably by jurisdiction, particularly between dealer and private-party sales, so understand what protections apply where you live.

## Don’t Be Afraid to Walk Away

One of the biggest advantages a used-car buyer has is the ability to leave.

Pressure to buy immediately, inconsistent answers about the vehicle’s history, missing paperwork, a refusal to permit an inspection, suspicious VIN or title issues, and unexplained warning lights are all reasons to reconsider the purchase.

A good used car doesn't have to be perfect. Normal cosmetic wear is expected, especially on an older vehicle. What matters is understanding its condition, history, likely future expenses, and whether the price reflects them.

Take your time, verify what the seller tells you, and spend a little money on an independent inspection. The best used-car deal isn't necessarily the cheapest car—it's the one whose condition, price, and ongoing costs make sense for you.
