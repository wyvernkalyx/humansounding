Subject: Burnout doesn’t disappear after a weekend off

Burnout can be surprisingly easy to normalize.

You start the week already tired. Small requests feel unusually irritating. Work that used to be satisfying becomes something to get through. You take a day off, feel a little better, and then find yourself right back where you started.

That’s because burnout usually isn’t just a problem of needing more sleep. It can develop when the demands of work consistently exceed the time, energy, control, or support you have available.

So if you’re feeling burned out, don’t make “be more productive” the first item on your recovery plan.

Start by identifying the source.

Maybe your workload is simply too high. Maybe priorities change constantly, leaving you with little sense of progress. Perhaps you’re always reachable, even after work. Or you could be putting enormous pressure on yourself to perform at a level that isn’t sustainable.

Once you know what’s draining you, look for changes that reduce the drain rather than merely helping you tolerate it.

A few places to start:

- Separate what truly needs to happen from what can wait, be delegated, or be dropped.
- Talk with your manager about priorities and tradeoffs. “I can finish A or B by Friday—which is more important?” is often more useful than quietly trying to do both.
- Create clearer boundaries around when you’re available, especially if work has started spilling into evenings and weekends.
- Take breaks and time off without treating them as rewards you have to earn.
- Pay attention to what restores your energy outside work: sleep, movement, relationships, hobbies, or simply unstructured time.

Most importantly, don’t treat burnout as a personal productivity failure. Individual habits matter, but they can’t fully compensate for chronic understaffing, unreasonable expectations, poor management, harassment, or a workplace culture that rewards overwork.

Sometimes the answer is better boundaries. Sometimes it’s a conversation about workload or accommodations. And sometimes, when circumstances allow, it’s looking for a different role or workplace.

Recovery may also take longer than you expect. If exhaustion, anxiety, low mood, sleep problems, or difficulty functioning are persistent or severe, consider talking with a healthcare or mental health professional.

One question worth carrying into this week:

What could change about the way I’m working—not just the way I’m coping—that would make this more sustainable?

You don’t need to solve everything at once. One meaningful change is a good place to begin.
