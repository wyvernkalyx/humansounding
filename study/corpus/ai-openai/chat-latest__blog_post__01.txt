# How to Choose a Password Manager

A good password manager solves a simple problem: you need strong, unique passwords for every account, but remembering dozens or hundreds of them is impractical. Instead of reusing passwords—or relying on predictable variations—a password manager creates, stores, and fills credentials for you.

Choosing one doesn’t need to be complicated. Here are the features that matter most.

## Start With Security

Your password manager will hold some of your most sensitive information, so its security model deserves scrutiny.

Look for a service that encrypts your vault so the provider cannot simply read its contents. Reputable providers should clearly document how encryption and key derivation work rather than asking users to trust vague claims about “military-grade encryption.”

Independent security audits are another useful signal. They don’t guarantee that software is vulnerability-free, but regular audits and a transparent approach to reporting and fixing security problems are encouraging.

You should also check what happens if you forget your master password. Recovery features can be valuable, particularly for families and businesses, but understand how they work and what trade-offs they introduce.

## Make Sure It Supports Strong Authentication

Your master password protects everything else, so choose a long, unique one and protect the account with multi-factor authentication when available.

Better password managers increasingly support passkeys and hardware security keys in addition to authenticator apps. These methods can provide strong protection against phishing. SMS-based verification is generally better than having no second factor at all, but it should not be your first choice when stronger alternatives are available.

## Consider Where You’ll Use It

A password manager that is frustrating to use tends to become a password manager you stop using.

Check compatibility with your computers, phones, browsers, and operating systems. Everyday features such as reliable autofill, automatic saving of new credentials, password generation, and synchronization across devices make a significant difference.

If you use multiple operating systems—for example, an Android phone and a Mac—test that combination before committing.

## Look Beyond Passwords

Modern password managers can often store more than usernames and passwords. Depending on your needs, useful features may include:

- Passkey storage and synchronization
- Secure notes and identity information
- Credit card storage
- Secure credential sharing
- Family or team vaults
- Emergency-access features
- Alerts about weak, reused, or exposed passwords
- Import and export tools

Don’t choose based on the longest feature list. Decide which capabilities you’ll actually use.

## Think About Portability

Switching services shouldn't mean rebuilding your digital life from scratch. Check whether the manager can import credentials from your browser or current password manager and, just as importantly, export your data in a usable format.

Keep in mind that exported vault files may be unencrypted. If you make one while migrating or backing up your credentials, protect it carefully and securely delete temporary copies when you're finished.

## Free, Paid, or Built In?

You don’t necessarily need to pay for a good password manager. Browsers and operating systems increasingly include capable credential managers, while dedicated products may offer broader cross-platform support, sharing tools, administrative features, or more control.

For many people, a reputable free option is enough. Paid plans become more compelling when you need family sharing, business management, advanced authentication options, additional encrypted storage, or support across a wider range of devices.

Price matters, but a low subscription fee is a poor bargain if the product makes exporting your data difficult or doesn’t work reliably on your devices.

## Research the Company, Too

No software is immune to security incidents. When evaluating a provider, look at its history, how clearly it documents its security architecture, whether it commissions independent audits, and how it has responded to past vulnerabilities or breaches.

A security incident isn't automatically proof that a product is unsafe forever. Conversely, a company with no widely reported breach isn't automatically safer. The quality of the underlying design and the provider's response to problems matter.

## Try Before You Commit

Once you have narrowed the field to two or three reputable options, install them and use them for a few days. Try logging in on different devices, creating a new account, changing a password, using autofill, and recovering access through the available recovery process.

The best password manager is ultimately one that combines a sound security design with software you will consistently use.

After choosing one, give every important account a unique, randomly generated password—or use a passkey where appropriate—and secure the password manager itself with strong authentication. That single change can eliminate one of the biggest risks in everyday account security: one stolen password unlocking several accounts.
