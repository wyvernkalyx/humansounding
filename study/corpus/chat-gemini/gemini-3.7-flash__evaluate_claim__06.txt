**Yes, that claim is largely true.** The idea that breakfast is the "most important meal of the day," along with the concept of modern breakfast foods, was largely engineered by the food and advertising industries in the 19th and 20th centuries. 

Here is the history behind how it happened:

---

### 1. The Literal Slogan: General Foods (1944)
The exact phrase gained widespread fame during a **1944 marketing campaign by General Foods** (the makers of Grape-Nuts cereal). 

To sell more cereal, the company ran radio ads and distributed pamphlets in grocery stores that declared: 
> *"Nutritionists say breakfast is the most important meal of the day."*

The campaign was a massive success, and the phrase quickly embedded itself into the American lexicon as an accepted medical fact, despite having little scientific backing at the time.

---

### 2. The Rise of Cereal: Kellogg and Post (Late 1800s–Early 1900s)
Before the late 19th century, breakfast in the West wasn't a standard, universally celebrated meal. People mostly ate leftovers, bread, or heavy meats. 

* **John Harvey Kellogg** (a deeply religious physician and Seventh-day Adventist) believed that a heavy, meat-filled diet caused indigestion, sinful thoughts, and moral decay. 
* He invented cornflakes at his Battle Creek Sanitarium as a bland, pre-packaged, healthy alternative to heavy breakfasts.
* **C.W. Post** (a former patient of Kellogg) copied the idea and launched Grape-Nuts, aggressively marketing breakfast cereal as a cure for fatigue and digestive issues.

Together, Kellogg and Post normalized the idea that everyone needed a specialized morning meal consisting of processed grains.

---

### 3. The "Bacon and Eggs" Campaign: Edward Bernays (1920s)
It wasn’t just cereal companies. The classic "bacon and eggs" breakfast was also the result of a deliberate PR campaign.

In the 1920s, the **Beech-Nut Packing Company** wanted to sell more bacon. They hired **Edward Bernays**, the "father of public relations." Bernays got a prominent doctor to state that a heavy breakfast was healthier than a light one because energy is lost overnight. He then polled roughly 4,500 other doctors, asking if they agreed that a hearty breakfast was good for health. 

Once thousands of doctors confirmed they agreed in theory, Bernays sent press releases to newspapers across the country framing "bacon and eggs" as a doctor-recommended, hearty breakfast. Bacon sales skyrocketed.

---

### What Does Modern Science Say?
Modern nutritional science shows that **breakfast is not universally the most important meal**:

1. **Industry-Funded Studies:** For decades, studies claiming that breakfast-skippers were more likely to be obese or perform worse in school were funded by companies like Kellogg, Quaker Oats, or General Mills.
2. **Correlation vs. Causation:** Many independent studies found that people who eat breakfast often have other healthy habits (they exercise more, smoke less, and sleep better), which made breakfast look like the cause of their good health ("healthy user bias").
3. **Individual Needs:** Current research shows that meal timing depends heavily on the individual. Practices like **intermittent fasting** (often involving skipping breakfast) have been shown to be perfectly healthy and effective for weight management and metabolic health for many people.

### Summary
While eating breakfast can be beneficial depending on your lifestyle, metabolism, and the quality of food you eat, the dogmatic belief that it is an absolute biological necessity—and the "most important meal"—was **born out of corporate advertising.**
