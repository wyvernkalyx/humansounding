To choose the right path, you need to look at three factors: **your financial runway**, **the nature of the work**, and **the management capacity of your current developer**. 

Going from one to two developers is a massive shift—it’s the moment you transition from a single person holding all the context to building an actual engineering organization.

Here is a framework to help you decide:

---

### Choose a **Full-Time Developer** if:
1. **The work is your core product:** You are building long-term intellectual property that will need constant iteration, maintenance, and refactoring.
2. **You need redundancy & shared ownership:** If your current developer gets hit by a bus (or burns out), your business halts. A second employee builds institutional memory, shares on-call duties, and cares about long-term architecture rather than just closing tickets.
3. **You have at least 12–18 months of runway:** Hiring an FTE is a fixed, long-term commitment. Factoring in recruiting, taxes, benefits, and equity, you need budget stability.
4. **The scope is evolving:** If you don't know exactly what features you'll be building 3 months from now, full-time employees are far better at navigating ambiguity than contractors.

---

### Choose **Contractors** if:
1. **The work is modular and well-defined:** You need a specific, bounded project done (e.g., a Shopify integration, a mobile app build, a design system implementation, or migrating a database).
2. **You need specialized expertise:** Your current dev is a great generalist/backend engineer, but you temporarily need a senior DevOps expert, a security auditor, or an iOS specialist.
3. **Runway/cash flow is volatile:** If you might need to cut expenses rapidly in 3–6 months, contractors turn fixed payroll into variable, easily pausable costs.
4. **You need speed immediately:** Sourcing and hiring a good full-time dev takes 2–4 months, plus 1–2 months to ramp up. A contractor can often start next Monday.

---

### The Hidden Trap: Contractor Management Overhead
If you only have **one** developer right now, bringing on contractors will **reduce your developer's individual output by 20% to 40%**. 

Contractors require:
* Detailed technical specifications.
* Architectural guardrails.
* Code reviews / PR approvals.
* QA and debugging integration issues.

If your current developer is already underwater, hiring contractors won't immediately relieve their stress—it might actually increase it in the short term.

---

### The Decision Matrix (Quick Test)

| Question | If Yes... | If No... |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Can you clearly define the deliverables down to the task level right now? | **Contractor** | **FTE** |
| Will this code need constant daily iteration 12 months from now? | **FTE** | **Contractor** |
| Is your runway less than 9 months? | **Contractor** | **FTE** |
| Does your current dev have time to write specs and review code? | **Contractor** | **FTE** |

---

### Recommended Strategy for Most Early-Stage Teams

If your budget allows, **start recruiting your 2nd Full-Time Developer now** (because hiring takes months), but **bring in a short-term contractor to knock out a specific, isolated bottleneck** while the hiring search is underway. 

Once the full-time hire is onboarded, phase out the contractor.
