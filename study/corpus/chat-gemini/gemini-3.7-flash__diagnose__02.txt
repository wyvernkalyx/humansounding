When interpersonal conflict escalates to the point of silence and affects work, **you must intervene immediately**. Passive-aggressive silence is toxic to team morale and project velocity. 

Your goal as a manager is not to make them like each other or become friends; **your goal is to establish functional, professional collaboration.**

Here is a 4-step framework to handle this:

---

### Step 1: Hold Separate 1-on-1s (Fact-Finding & Framing)
Meet with each person individually first. Do not put them in a room together until you understand both sides.

* **Acknowledge the observable behavior and business impact:**
  > *"I’ve noticed that communication between you and [Name] has completely stopped, and it’s starting to impact [Project/Deliverable/Team]. What’s going on from your perspective?"*
* **Listen actively, but keep them focused on facts:**
  * Let them vent briefly, but redirect personal attacks to specific incidents: *"What specific action or event triggered this?"*
* **Set the baseline expectation:**
  * Make it clear that silence is not an acceptable workplace behavior: 
  > *"You do not have to be friends or spend time together outside of work. However, effective, respectful communication on work matters is a non-negotiable requirement of your role."*
* **Prepare them for a joint meeting:**
  * Ask: *"What is one thing you need from [Name] to get the work back on track?"* and *"What are you willing to do to fix this?"*

---

### Step 2: The Facilitated 3-Way Meeting
Once you know both perspectives, bring them together. You are the mediator and the boundary-setter.

* **Set Ground Rules upfront:**
  * No interrupting or talking over each other.
  * Focus on the work and specific behaviors, not personality traits.
  * Focus on future solutions, not relitigating past grievances.
* **State the shared objective:**
  > *"We are here today because the lack of communication is hurting the project and the team. We need to walk out of this room with a clear agreement on how you two will collaborate moving forward."*
* **Have each person state their work-related needs:**
  * Ask Person A: *"What do you need from Person B to do your job effectively?"*
  * Ask Person B: *"What do you need from Person A to do your job effectively?"*
* **Address the friction point:**
  * If it’s a process issue (e.g., missed handoffs, unclear ownership), clarify roles on the spot.
  * If it’s a communication style clash, help them agree on standard protocols.

---

### Step 3: Establish Concrete Working Protocols
Do not leave the meeting with vague promises like "we'll try to get along." Define explicit, objective operational rules:

1. **Channels:** Where will communication happen? (e.g., *"All project handoffs must happen in the shared Slack channel or via Jira, not DMs."*)
2. **Response Times:** How quickly must they reply to work requests? (e.g., *"Acknowledge work requests within 4 hours during business hours."*)
3. **Escalation Path:** What happens when they disagree on a technical or strategic decision? (e.g., *"If you can't reach consensus within 2 hours, bring the two options to me for a final call—do not let the task stall."*)

---

### Step 4: Document, Monitor, and Enforce
* **Send a recap email:** Document the agreed-upon protocols to both of them so there is no ambiguity.
* **Set a check-in date:** Schedule a 15-minute follow-up with each person in 1–2 weeks to see how the agreement is holding up.
* **Treat communication as a performance metric:** 
  * If one or both continue the silent treatment or sabotage work, treat it as a **conduct/performance issue**, not a personal dispute. 
  * Involve HR if necessary, and make the consequences clear: ongoing refusal to collaborate will lead to formal performance management.

---

### Summary Rule of Thumb
* **Don't ask them:** *"Why can't you two just get along?"* (Invites emotional venting)
* **Do ask them:** *"What specific processes do we need to put in place so the work gets done smoothly?"* (Focuses on professional accountability)
