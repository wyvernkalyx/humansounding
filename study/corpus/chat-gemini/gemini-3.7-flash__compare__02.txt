For someone who relocates every four years, **renting is almost always the financially superior and less stressful choice.** 

A traditional rule of thumb is that it takes **5 to 7 years** of staying in one home to break even on the upfront and back-end costs of homeownership. 

Here is a detailed comparison of why a four-year cycle makes buying difficult, the math behind it, and the few exceptions where buying might make sense.

---

### 1. The Financial Breakdown (The 4-Year Math)

#### A. Transaction Costs (The "Friction" Killer)
Buying and selling real estate incurs massive overhead:
* **When Buying:** Closing costs, loan origination fees, appraisal, and title insurance run **2% to 4%** of the purchase price.
* **When Selling:** Agent commissions, transfer taxes, seller concessions, and title fees run **6% to 10%** of the sale price.
* **Total Cost:** Roughly **8% to 14% of the home's value** vanishes in transaction fees every four years. 
> *Example:* On a \$400,000 home, you lose roughly **\$35,000 to \$50,000** just in fees every four years. To simply break even, the home must appreciate roughly 3% to 4% every single year just to cover friction costs.

#### B. The Amortization Trap
In the first four years of a standard 30-year fixed mortgage, the vast majority of your monthly payment goes toward **interest, property taxes, and insurance**, not principal.
* After four years, you will have paid off only about **3% to 5% of the total loan balance**. 
* You build very little equity through debt paydown in that timeframe.

#### C. Maintenance and Short-Term Capital Expenditures
When you own, you are on the hook for major systems (HVAC, roof, plumbing, water heater). If a \$10,000 HVAC unit dies in year two, you cannot stay long enough to extract the value of that replacement before you sell.

#### D. Opportunity Cost
The down payment (often 5% to 20%), closing costs, and cash reserves are tied up in an illiquid asset. If invested in a diversified index fund (like the S&P 500), that capital is completely liquid and has historically offered competitive returns with zero maintenance stress.

### 2. Side-by-Side Comparison

| Feature | Renting (4-Year Cycle) | Buying & Selling (4-Year Cycle) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Financial Predictability** | **High.** Fixed monthly rent; no surprise repair bills. | **Low.** Unplanned repairs, property tax hikes, HOA increases. |
| **Flexibility / Exit** | **Effortless.** Give 30–60 days' notice when the lease ends. | **Difficult.** Requires staging, listing, showings, negotiations, and closing. |
| **Market Risk** | **Zero.** If the housing market crashes, you walk away unaffected. | **High.** A local downturn in year 3 or 4 could leave you underwater or forced to sell at a loss. |
| **Wealth Building** | Only through disciplined investing of the money *not* spent on homeownership costs. | Highly dependent on rapid home appreciation to offset fees. |
| **Customization** | Limited; cannot significantly alter the space. | Complete freedom to modify the home. |

---

### 3. The Exception: "Buy and Hold" (Building a Rental Portfolio)

The one scenario where buying every four years works is **if you don’t sell**.

If you move every four years (common for military personnel, consultants, or corporate climbers), you could adopt a **buy-and-hold rental strategy**:
1. Buy Home #1 with an owner-occupant mortgage (lower interest rate/down payment).
2. Live in it for 4 years.
3. Move to City #2, rent out Home #1 to a tenant, and buy Home #2.
4. Repeat.

**However, this comes with major caveats:**
* **Cash Flow Risk:** Rent must exceed the mortgage, taxes, insurance, vacancy allowance, and property management fees (typically 8–10% of monthly rent).
* **Long-Distance Landlording:** Managing properties across multiple states or cities is complex and can be stressful.
* **Financing Limits:** Lenders will eventually scrutinize your debt-to-income (DTI) ratio if the rental properties aren't showing consistent, documented net profit on your tax returns.

---

### Final Recommendation

* **Rent if:** You want low stress, predictable costs, liquidity, and you don't want to become a landlord. Over a 4-year period, **renting is the mathematically safer and financially superior choice.**
* **Buy if:** You are in a rapidly booming market, plan to keep the home as a long-term cash-flowing rental property when you leave, and have the capital reserves to handle multiple mortgages and remote property management.
