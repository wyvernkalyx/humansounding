The fundamental difference between a condo and a co-op comes down to **what you actually own**. 

* In a **Condo**, you own **real property** (the specific physical unit, plus a fraction of the shared common areas). You get a deed, just like buying a single-family home.
* In a **Co-op** (Housing Cooperative), you own **shares in a corporation** that owns the entire building. Your shares give you a "proprietary lease" that grants you the right to live in a specific unit.

Because of this legal difference, the day-to-day and financial reality for an owner differs dramatically in several key ways:

---

### 1. The Buying and Approval Process
* **Co-op:** The buying process is notoriously intrusive. The co-op board has near-absolute power to approve or reject a buyer. You must submit exhaustive financial records (tax returns, bank statements, reference letters) and attend an in-person interview. The board can reject a buyer for almost any non-discriminatory reason (e.g., they think your finances are too tight, or they simply don't like your lifestyle) and **they do not have to give a reason**.
* **Condo:** The approval process is mostly a formality. Condos usually only have a "right of first refusal"—meaning if they don't want you to buy the unit, the condo board itself must purchase it at the agreed-upon price (which almost never happens). If you have the money and the seller accepts your offer, you will almost certainly close.

### 2. Financing and Down Payments
* **Co-op:** Boards typically require much higher down payments—often **20% to 50%**, with some high-end co-ops requiring 100% cash. They also enforce strict **Debt-to-Income (DTI)** ratios (often under 25–28%) and require **"post-closing liquidity"** (e.g., you must have 1–2 years’ worth of mortgage and maintenance payments left in the bank *after* closing).
* **Condo:** Financing is much more flexible. Standard conventional loans apply, often allowing down payments of **10% or even less**, depending on the lender. Post-closing liquidity is dictated by your bank, not the building.

### 3. Monthly Fees and Property Taxes
* **Co-op (Maintenance Fee):** You pay one single monthly fee that covers:
  1. The building’s operating expenses (maintenance, staff, trash).
  2. **The building's property taxes** (the co-op pays the city, not you individually).
  3. The interest on the building’s underlying master mortgage (if the building has one).
* **Condo (HOA Fee + Separate Tax Bill):** You pay two separate bills:
  1. A monthly Common Charge/HOA fee (operations, amenities, staff).
  2. An **individual property tax bill** sent directly to you by the municipality, based on your specific unit's assessed value.

*Note: Co-op monthly fees usually look higher on paper, but only because they bundle in the property taxes that condo owners pay separately.*

### 4. Upfront Costs vs. Closing Costs
* **Co-op:** 
  * **Cheaper purchase price:** Co-ops typically cost 10% to 30% less per square foot than comparable condos.
  * **Lower closing costs:** Because a co-op is technically personal property (shares) and not real estate, you avoid certain real estate closing costs like Title Insurance and Mortgage Recording Tax (in states that have it, like New York).
* **Condo:** 
  * **Higher purchase price:** You pay a premium for the flexibility and ownership structure.
  * **Higher closing costs:** Because it is real property, closing costs can be thousands of dollars higher.

### 5. Renting and Subletting Rules (Flexibility)
* **Co-op:** Heavily restricted. Co-ops want communities of owner-occupants, not transient tenants. Many buildings have "two out of five years" sublet rules, meaning you can only rent your unit out for a total of two years during any five-year period. Some co-ops forbid renting entirely.
* **Condo:** Investor-friendly. While some condos have minimum lease durations (e.g., no leases under 6 months), you can generally rent out your unit indefinitely with minimal board interference. 

### 6. Selling and Liquidity
* **Co-op:** Harder and slower to sell. Because your future buyer must also pass the board's strict financial scrutiny, your buyer pool is smaller. Many co-ops also charge a **"flip tax"**—a fee (often 1% to 3% of the gross sale price or profit) paid back to the co-op reserve fund when you sell.
* **Condo:** Faster and easier to sell. Your buyer pool includes foreign buyers, investors, parents buying for adult children, and people using low-down-payment mortgages. 

---

### Summary: Which is better?

| Feature | Co-op | Condo |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Legal Status** | Shares in a corporation | Real estate / Deed |
| **Purchase Price** | Generally lower | Generally higher |
| **Board Scrutiny** | Extreme (can reject you) | Minimal |
| **Down Payment** | High (usually 20%–50%+) | Flexible (often 10%–20%) |
| **Renting it Out** | Highly restricted | Easy / Flexible |
| **Ideal For...** | Long-term primary residents seeking value | Investors, frequent movers, or those who want autonomy |
