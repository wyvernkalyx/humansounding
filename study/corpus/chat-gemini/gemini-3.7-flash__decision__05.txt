Deciding whether to rebuild or continue patching a 10-year-old internal tool is one of the most classic (and expensive) dilemmas in software engineering. A full rewrite often takes twice as long as estimated, but nursing legacy code can quietly bleed engineering hours and employee productivity.

To make the right choice, run your tool through this **4-part decision framework**.

---

### 1. The Diagnostic: When to Rebuild vs. When to Patch

#### **You should REBUILD if:**
* **Development velocity has flatlined:** Adding a minor feature takes weeks instead of hours because the codebase is brittle (“spaghetti code”) and changes cause unpredictable side effects.
* **The tech stack is an active liability:** The framework, runtime, or operating system is end-of-life (EOL), posing critical **security vulnerabilities** or making it impossible to hire developers willing to work on it.
* **It blocks core business growth:** The tool cannot scale to handle current data volumes, lacks APIs required to integrate with modern software, or forces manual workarounds that slow down the business.
* **The UX actively hurts operations:** Employees spend hours doing tasks that should take minutes, or onboarding new staff requires weeks of training just to navigate the tool.

#### **You should KEEP PATCHING if:**
* **The business logic is extremely complex and undocumented:** Decades of edge cases are baked into the code, and nobody fully understands the business rules anymore. A rewrite risks breaking critical, forgotten workflows.
* **The tool is "stable enough":** It does its job, requires minimal maintenance (e.g., less than a few days a quarter), and isn't blocking strategic initiatives.
* **You don’t have dedicated resources:** If you can only afford to dedicate half a developer part-time to the rebuild, it will never ship. A rebuild requires focused, dedicated capacity.

---

### 2. The Hidden Alternatives (Don't just think "Patch vs. Rewrite")

Before committing to a multi-month custom rebuild, consider these two alternatives:

#### Alternative A: Buy or Use Low-Code
Ten years ago, you *had* to build custom tools from scratch. Today, that is rarely true.
* Can modern SaaS replace 80% of this tool's functionality?
* Can modern internal-tool platforms (like **Retool, Appsmith, Superblocks**) let you rebuild the UI in weeks on top of your existing databases and APIs, bypassing standard front-end development?

#### Alternative B: The "Strangler Fig" Approach (Incremental Migration)
Instead of a "Big Bang" rewrite (which has a high failure rate), replace the old tool piece by piece:
1. Build a modern API layer in front of the legacy database.
2. Build new features in a modern system.
3. Migrate the most painful legacy workflows one at a time.
4. Decommission the old tool only when the last module is moved.

---

### 3. Quick ROI Calculation

To justify the decision to leadership, calculate the **Cost of the Status Quo** vs. the **Cost of Rebuilding**:

$$\text{Annual Cost of Patching} = (\text{Dev Maintenance Hours} \times \text{Rate}) + (\text{User Lost Productivity Hours} \times \text{Rate}) + \text{Cost of Errors/Downtime}$$

If the annual cost of patching (plus lost productivity) is greater than **30–50% of the estimated cost of a rebuild**, a replacement usually pays for itself within 2 to 3 years.

---

### Recommended Next Step

Run a **1-week discovery spike**:
1. **Audit the Users:** Spend 30 minutes shadowing the primary users of the tool. Note their biggest daily frustrations.
2. **Audit the Code:** Have a senior engineer identify the 3 worst architectural bottlenecks and any security/EOL dependencies.
3. **Prototype a Low-Code Alternative:** Spend two days building a proof-of-concept for *one* workflow using a low-code tool or standard modern framework. 

This will give you the actual data needed to commit to a direction with confidence.
