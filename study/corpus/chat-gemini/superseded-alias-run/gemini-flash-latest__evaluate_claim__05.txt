The short answer is **no, it is not the single most common reason, but it is easily one of the top three—and often the hidden root cause behind failures attributed to other things.**

Here is what the data actually says, where that common claim comes from, and why founder conflict is deadlier than standard statistics suggest.

---

### 1. What the Data Actually Says
Most aggregate post-mortem studies (like those by **CB Insights** and **Failory**) consistently rank reasons for startup failure in this order:

1. **No Market Need / Lack of Product-Market Fit (~35–40%):** Building something nobody actually wants or is willing to pay for.
2. **Running Out of Cash / Failed to Raise Capital (~38%):** Simply burning through runway before becoming profitable or fundable.
3. **Team / Cofounder Issues (~14–18%):** Misalignment, lack of complementary skills, or toxic interpersonal dynamics.
4. **Getting Outcompeted (~20%):** Larger or faster players taking the market.

By pure numbers, **building the wrong product** and **running out of money** statistically kill more startups than cofounder fighting.

---

### 2. Where Does the "Most Startups" Myth Come From?
The claim usually traces back to **Noam Wasserman**, a Harvard Business School professor and author of *The Founder's Dilemmas*. 

Wasserman’s research found that **65% of high-potential startups fail due to cofounder conflict.** 

However, there is a nuance: Wasserman was specifically looking at failures driven by *internal "people problems"* (e.g., equity splits, decision-making, role clarity), not necessarily macroeconomic or market failures. Over time, that nuance got lost in popular business media, morphing into the headline: *"65% of all startups fail because of cofounder drama."*

---

### 3. Why Cofounder Conflict is the "Hidden Killer"
Even if it ranks 3rd or 4th on official autopsy reports, venture capitalists (including Y Combinator) often view cofounder conflict as the most fatal underlying disease. Here’s why:

* **It masquerades as a "Cash" problem:** If cofounders disagree on product strategy, decision-making slows down. Instead of shipping an update in two weeks, it takes three months. That wasted time burns cash. When the company dies, the post-mortem reads *"Ran out of money,"* but the actual disease was *founder gridlock*.
* **It ruins pivots:** Great startups often fail their first three ideas before finding the winning one. Making a radical pivot requires absolute trust and speed. When cofounders are fighting, they cannot execute a pivot, and the startup sinks with the original bad idea.
* **Investors run away:** VCs expect messy code and unproven markets, but they almost never invest in a founding team that exhibits passive aggression, unresolved equity battles, or communication breakdown. 

---

### 4. The Most Common Cofounder Breaking Points
When startups do die from founder conflict, it usually stems from four specific errors made in the first few months:

1. **The "Handshake" Equity Split:** Splitting equity 50/50 without a 4-year vesting schedule. If one founder quits after 6 months, they walk away with half the company, making the startup un-investable for future VCs.
2. **Mismatched Commitment/Risk Tolerance:** One founder works 80 hours a week and quit their job; the other treats it like a side project and wants to keep a stable salary.
3. **Role Overlap / Ego:** Two technical founders or two business founders fighting over who gets to make the final call on product or strategy.
4. **Diverging Values:** One founder wants to build a small, profitable lifestyle business; the other wants to raise $50M and try to build a billion-dollar monopoly.

### Bottom Line
Statistically, **building a product nobody wants** is what kills most startups. But **cofounder conflict** is the most lethal internal failure mode—largely because a cohesive team can survive a failed product and pivot, but a great product rarely survives a broken team.
