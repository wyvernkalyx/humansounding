The fundamental difference between a condo and a co-op comes down to **what you legally own**. That single difference completely changes your buying process, monthly finances, flexibility, and daily life as a resident.

Here is a breakdown of how the two differ from an owner’s perspective.

---

### 1. What You Actually Own
* **Condo (Real Property):** You own your individual unit outright (the "airspace" inside your walls) and receive a **real estate deed**, just like buying a single-family home. You also own an undivided fractional share of the building’s common areas (hallways, roof, lobby).
* **Co-op (Personal Property / Shares):** You do *not* own real estate. Instead, you own **shares in a corporation** that owns the building. In exchange for your shares, the corporation grants you a **proprietary lease** (or occupancy agreement) allowing you to live in a specific unit.

---

### 2. The Board and the Approval Process
* **Condo (Low Gatekeeping):** 
  * The board has very little power to reject buyers. They usually only hold a "right of first refusal" (meaning if they don't want you to buy it, the board must buy the unit themselves, which rarely happens).
  * The application process is generally straightforward and faster.
* **Co-op (High Gatekeeping):** 
  * The board has massive control over who enters the building. 
  * You will undergo intense financial scrutiny (often submitting years of tax returns, bank statements, personal and professional reference letters).
  * You must pass a formal in-person or virtual **board interview**.
  * The board can legally reject a buyer for almost any reason (or no reason at all), provided they don’t violate federal Fair Housing laws.

---

### 3. Financial Requirements and Upfront Costs
* **Condo:**
  * **Purchase Price:** Typically 10%–30% higher than a comparable co-op.
  * **Down Payment:** More flexible. Often allows as little as 5% to 10% down (though 20% is common).
  * **Closing Costs:** Higher, because you have to pay for title insurance and mortgage recording taxes (in states that have them).
* **Co-op:**
  * **Purchase Price:** Generally cheaper per square foot than condos.
  * **Down Payment:** Much stricter. Most co-ops require a minimum of 20% to 30% down, and some exclusive buildings require 50% down or all-cash.
  * **Post-Closing Liquidity:** Co-ops often require you to have 1 to 2 years' worth of monthly maintenance and mortgage payments left over in liquid cash/assets after closing.
  * **Closing Costs:** Lower, because there is no title insurance or mortgage recording tax (it’s a share loan, not a traditional mortgage).

---

### 4. Monthly Fees and Property Taxes
* **Condo:**
  * You pay a **Common Charge / HOA Fee** for building maintenance, insurance, and amenities.
  * You receive a **separate property tax bill** directly from the local government for your specific unit.
* **Co-op:**
  * You pay a single **Maintenance Fee**. This includes your share of the building's operating costs, **plus your portion of the entire building’s property taxes**, and sometimes the payment toward the building’s single underlying master mortgage.

---

### 5. Renting, Subletting, and Investment
* **Condo (Investor-Friendly):**
  * You can generally rent out your unit with few restrictions (though some buildings may require minimum 6-month or 1-year leases).
  * Ideal if you plan to move out eventually and keep the unit as a rental property.
* **Co-op (Owner-Occupant Focused):**
  * Extremely strict sublet rules. Many co-ops forbid renting entirely, or only allow you to sublet for a maximum of 1 to 2 years out of every 5 years (and only after you have lived there yourself for a few years).
  * Subtenants must often be vetted and approved by the co-op board.

---

### 6. Renovations and Selling
* **Condo:**
  * You need permits and standard building approval, but rules regarding remodeling are generally reasonable.
  * Selling is easy: Once you find a qualified buyer, the deal will almost certainly close.
* **Co-op:**
  * Alteration agreements are often very strict (some limit renovation work to certain months of the year).
  * Selling takes longer: A buyer must be found, submit a board package, pass the interview, and be approved. If rejected, you have to start over.
  * Many co-ops charge a **"flip tax"** when you sell—a transfer fee (e.g., 1% to 3% of the gross sale price or a percentage of the profit) that goes back into the building’s reserve fund.

---

### Summary Comparison Table

| Feature | Condominium (Condo) | Housing Cooperative (Co-op) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **What you own** | Real Estate (Deed) | Shares in a Corporation (Lease) |
| **Purchase Price** | Higher | Lower |
| **Down Payment** | Flexible (5%–20%) | Strict (usually 20%–30%+) |
| **Monthly Fees Cover** | Building upkeep only (Taxes billed separately) | Upkeep + Property Taxes + Master Mortgage |
| **Board Power** | Low (Minimal approval power) | High (Can reject buyers/subtenants) |
| **Subletting / Renting** | Generally allowed / Easy | Heavily restricted or prohibited |
| **Best For** | Investors, frequent movers, foreign buyers | Long-term primary residents seeking value |
