Broaching property issues with neighbors is tricky, but it can be done smoothly if you frame it around **mutual safety and property protection** rather than placing blame. 

Here is a step-by-step game plan, including exact scripts to use:

---

### 1. The Right Mindset & Timing
* **Don't do it in the heat of the moment:** Never approach them while actively dragging a heavy branch off your lawn in the rain. Wait for a calm, clear day.
* **Assume they don’t know:** Trees look very different from the trunk side versus the overhang side. They genuinely might not realize how far the branches extend or how dead some of them are.
* **Talk in person:** A text, letter, or note can easily be misread as passive-aggressive. A casual, face-to-face conversation sets a friendly tone.

---

### 2. The Conversation Script
The goal is to frame the tree—not the neighbor—as the problem, and position the two of you as a team solving a safety issue.

> **The Casual Opener:**
> *"Hey [Neighbor's Name], got a quick minute? I wanted to check in about that big [oak/maple/tree] along our fence line."*

> **The Observation (No Blame):**
> *"During the last couple of storms, we’ve had some pretty large branches come down in the yard. Luckily nobody was hurt, but with storm season picking up, I’m getting a little worried that a bigger one might take out the fence, a car, or damage a roof."*

> **The Collaborative Ask:**
> *"I was thinking about having an arborist come out to look at trimming back the dead wood over my side. Would you be open to having them look at the whole tree while they’re here, or do you have a tree service you usually prefer to work with?"*

---

### 3. Smart Compromises to Keep the Peace
* **Offer property access:** Let them know that if they hire a tree trimmer, the crew is welcome to work from your yard to make the job easier/cheaper.
* **Consider splitting the cost:** Legally, in most jurisdictions, you are allowed to trim branches extending over your property line at your own expense (as long as it doesn't harm the health of the tree). Offering to split the cost of a professional arborist to prune the whole tree shows extreme goodwill and usually gets a fast "yes."
* **Offer to handle debris together:** *"Next time a storm hits, if there’s a mess, let me know if you need a hand with the cleanup on your side, too."*

---

### 4. What NOT to Do
* **Don’t throw the branches back over the fence.** Even if it’s legally "their" tree, tossing debris into their yard is the fastest way to start a neighborhood cold war.
* **Don’t start with legal threats.** Avoid phrases like *"You are liable if this hits my roof"* during the first conversation. Save legalities as an absolute last resort if they become combative or negligent.
* **Don’t hack the tree yourself carelessly.** In many areas, if you trim overhanging branches so aggressively that the tree gets diseased or dies, you could actually be held financially liable for the value of the mature tree.

---

### 5. If They Are Completely Unreceptive
If they shrug it off and refuse to do anything:
1. **Take photos** of the dead branches in the canopy and any fallen limbs after storms to establish a pattern.
2. **Exercise your legal right** (check local municipal codes first): Hire a certified, insured arborist to cleanly prune only the branches that cross your property line.
3. If the entire tree is clearly dead/rotting and poses an imminent threat to your home, you may eventually need to send a certified, polite letter notifying them of the hazard—this establishes a paper trail for insurance/negligence purposes—but only cross that bridge if a friendly conversation fails.
