### 1. The Brain is an Energy-Saving Machine

To understand habit change, you have to understand why habits exist in the first place: **Survival.**

Your brain makes up about 2% of your body weight but consumes roughly 20% of your energy. To prevent cognitive burnout, the brain turns repeated behaviors into automatic routines managed by the *basal ganglia* (the primitive part of your brain). 

When a behavior becomes a habit, your prefrontal cortex (the part responsible for decision-making and willpower) essentially goes to sleep.

**The takeaway:** Willpower is a high-energy, finite resource. If your strategy relies on daily willpower, you are biologically wired to fail when you get tired, stressed, or hungry. 

---

### 2. The Power of "Identity-Based" Habits

Most people try to change their habits by focusing on **outcomes**:
* *“I want to lose 15 pounds.”*
* *“I want to read 30 books this year.”*

Psychologist and author James Clear points out that the deepest level of behavior change is **identity-based**. 

* Outcome: *“I am trying to run a marathon.”*
* Identity: *“I am a runner.”*

When your behavior aligns with who you believe you are, you no longer need willpower. You aren’t forcing yourself to read; you are simply acting like a reader. 

Every action you take is a vote for the type of person you wish to become. You don’t need a landslide victory—you just need a simple majority.

---

### 3. Lower the "Activation Energy"

In chemistry, *activation energy* is the minimum amount of energy required to initiate a chemical reaction. In human psychology, it’s the mental effort required to start a task.

Human behavior follows the Law of Least Effort: **We naturally gravitate toward the option that requires the least amount of friction.**

If you want to build a good habit, reduce the friction:
* Want to play guitar? Leave it on a stand in the living room, not in the closet inside its case.
* Want to eat healthier? Cut up the fruit and put it at eye level in the fridge.

If you want to break a bad habit, increase the friction:
* Put your phone in another room while working.
* Log out of social media apps after every session so you have to type your password to get back in.

Small physical barriers create massive psychological speed bumps.

---

### The 2-Rule Habit Toolkit

If you’re trying to build a new habit this week, use these two evidence-based psychological rules:

#### **Rule 1: The 2-Minute Rule**
When starting a new habit, scale it down until it takes less than two minutes to do.
* *“Read 30 pages a day”* becomes *“Read one page.”*
* *“Do 45 minutes of yoga”* becomes *“Unroll my yoga mat.”*

A habit must be established before it can be improved. Master the art of simply showing up.

#### **Rule 2: Never Miss Twice**
Perfectionism is the enemy of consistency. The psychological phenomenon known as the *"What-the-Hell Effect"* happens when we break a streak once, feel like a failure, and abandon the habit entirely.

Missing once is an accident. Missing twice is the start of a new, bad habit. If you miss a workout today, do whatever it takes to do even a 5-minute stretch tomorrow. Protect the identity.
