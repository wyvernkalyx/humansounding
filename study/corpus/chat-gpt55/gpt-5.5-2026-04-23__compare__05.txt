For the person living there, the biggest difference is **what you actually own**.

## Condo: you own real estate
With a **condominium**, you typically own your individual unit as real property, plus a shared interest in common areas like hallways, roof, gym, lobby, etc.

### What that means for you
- **You get a deed** to your unit.
- You usually get a **mortgage secured by the unit**.
- You pay:
  - Your mortgage, if any
  - Property taxes, often billed directly to you
  - Condo association/HOA fees
- You are responsible for the inside of your unit.
- The condo association handles shared building expenses.
- Selling is usually more straightforward, though the buyer may still need to meet association rules.
- Renting out the unit is often easier than in a co-op, though some condos restrict rentals.

## Co-op: you own shares in a corporation
With a **co-op**, you usually do **not** own your apartment directly. Instead, you own shares in a corporation that owns the building. Those shares give you a proprietary lease or occupancy agreement allowing you to live in a specific unit.

### What that means for you
- You do **not get a deed** to the apartment itself.
- You own **shares** tied to the unit.
- Your loan, if any, is usually a **share loan**, not a traditional mortgage.
- You pay a monthly **maintenance fee** to the co-op corporation.
- That maintenance fee often includes:
  - Building expenses
  - Property taxes
  - Sometimes underlying building mortgage payments
  - Sometimes utilities
- The co-op board often has more control over sales, sublets, renovations, and who can buy.
- Selling can require board approval of the buyer.
- Renting/subletting is often more restricted.

## Practical differences for an owner

| Issue | Condo | Co-op |
|---|---|---|
| What you own | The unit itself | Shares in the building corporation |
| Legal document | Deed | Stock certificate/proprietary lease |
| Financing | Traditional mortgage | Share loan |
| Monthly payments | HOA/common charges + taxes | Maintenance fee, often includes taxes |
| Board control | Usually less | Usually more |
| Selling | Generally easier | Often requires board approval |
| Renting out | Often more flexible | Often restricted |
| Taxes | Paid directly in many cases | Often paid through maintenance |
| Buyer approval | Usually limited | Often extensive |
| Common in | Many markets | Especially NYC and some older urban markets |

## The biggest lifestyle difference
A **condo** tends to feel more like owning a house inside a shared building: more individual control and easier resale.

A **co-op** tends to feel more like joining a private residential corporation: often stricter rules, more board oversight, and potentially lower purchase prices, but less flexibility.

## Which is better?
Neither is automatically better.

A **condo** may be better if you want:
- Easier financing
- Easier resale
- More flexibility to rent
- More individual control

A **co-op** may be better if you want:
- Potentially lower purchase price
- A more controlled building community
- Possibly lower closing costs in some markets
- A board that screens buyers and may protect building stability

The exact rules vary a lot by building, so the governing documents and board policies matter as much as the label.
