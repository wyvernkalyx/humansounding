In the United States, a bill becomes law through several steps involving Congress and the President:

1. **A bill is introduced**
   - A member of Congress introduces a proposed law, called a bill.
   - Bills can start in either the **House of Representatives** or the **Senate**, except tax and spending bills, which must start in the House.

2. **Committee review**
   - The bill is sent to a congressional committee that specializes in the bill’s topic.
   - The committee studies it, holds hearings, makes changes, and decides whether to send it forward.
   - If the committee rejects or ignores the bill, it usually dies.

3. **Debate and vote in one chamber**
   - The full House or Senate debates the bill.
   - Members may propose amendments.
   - If a majority votes in favor, the bill moves to the other chamber.

4. **The other chamber considers the bill**
   - The bill goes through a similar process in the other chamber: committee review, debate, amendments, and a vote.
   - Both the House and Senate must pass the same version of the bill.

5. **Conference committee, if needed**
   - If the House and Senate pass different versions, a conference committee works out a compromise.
   - Both chambers must then vote to approve the final version.

6. **The President acts**
   - Once Congress passes the bill, it goes to the President.
   - The President can:
     - **Sign it**, making it law.
     - **Veto it**, sending it back to Congress.
     - **Do nothing** for 10 days while Congress is in session, in which case it becomes law.
     - **Do nothing** for 10 days after Congress has adjourned, causing a “pocket veto,” and the bill does not become law.

7. **Congress can override a veto**
   - If the President vetoes the bill, Congress can still make it law with a **two-thirds vote** in both the House and the Senate.

In short: a bill must pass both houses of Congress and either be signed by the President or survive a veto override to become law.
