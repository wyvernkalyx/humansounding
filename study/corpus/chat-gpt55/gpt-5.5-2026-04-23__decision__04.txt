It *can* be worth moving a small office to a four-day week, but it depends on whether you can maintain client/service coverage and measure output clearly. For many small offices, the best approach is a **trial period** rather than a permanent switch.

## Potential benefits

- **Recruitment and retention:** A four-day week is a strong perk, especially if salaries stay the same.
- **Productivity gains:** Shorter weeks often force better prioritization, fewer unnecessary meetings, and more focused work.
- **Lower burnout:** Staff may return more rested, reducing absenteeism and turnover.
- **Cost savings:** Possible savings on utilities, office supplies, commuting support, or facilities usage.
- **Employer brand:** It can differentiate your business.

## Main risks

- **Client coverage gaps:** If customers expect five-day availability, closing one day may hurt service.
- **Work compression:** Staff may feel pressured to do five days of work in four, increasing stress.
- **Uneven workload:** Some roles may adapt easily; others may not.
- **Coordination problems:** Fewer overlapping hours can slow approvals or team communication.
- **Perceived unfairness:** If some employees can take four days and others cannot, morale may suffer.

## Key decision: which model?

There are two common versions:

### 1. True four-day week
Employees work around **32 hours for the same pay**, with the expectation that productivity is maintained.

Best if:
- Work is output-based.
- You can reduce low-value meetings/admin.
- Staff have autonomy.
- Client coverage can be managed.

### 2. Compressed workweek
Employees work **four longer days**, e.g. 4 x 10-hour days.

Best if:
- You need the same total hours.
- Your work requires coverage rather than pure output.
- You can manage fatigue from longer days.

This is easier operationally but may be less beneficial for morale and burnout.

## Options for a small office

Instead of closing the whole office every Friday, consider:

- **Staggered days off:** Half the team off Monday, half Friday.
- **Core coverage:** Office remains open five days, but each person works four.
- **Every-other-Friday off:** A softer transition.
- **Seasonal trial:** Four-day weeks during quieter months.
- **No-meeting day:** Try reducing meetings before reducing days.

## How to test it

Run a **3-month pilot** with clear rules.

Track:

- Revenue or billable output
- Customer response times
- Project completion rates
- Employee satisfaction
- Sick days/absences
- Overtime or after-hours work
- Missed deadlines
- Client complaints

Before the pilot, define what “success” means. For example:

> “We will continue the four-day week if revenue, customer response times, and project deadlines remain within 95% of current levels, while employee satisfaction improves.”

## Practical rules to set

- Which day is off?
- Is pay unchanged?
- Are hours reduced or compressed?
- How will urgent issues be handled?
- Are clients told?
- What happens during holidays?
- Are part-time staff affected?
- Can the arrangement be revoked if performance drops?

## My recommendation

If your office does knowledge work, admin, creative, finance, consulting, or project-based work, a **three-month pilot of a true 32-hour four-day week** may be worth trying.

If you have customer-facing operations that require daily coverage, consider a **staggered four-day schedule** rather than closing one day entirely.

The biggest mistake is announcing it permanently without testing. The best path is to pilot it, measure results, and adjust based on evidence.
