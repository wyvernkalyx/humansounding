Plenty—temps can help, but a “two temps for a month” plan can fail if the backlog problem is not mostly simple, repeatable work.

Here’s what could go wrong:

1. **Training takes longer than expected**
   - Your existing team may spend so much time onboarding, reviewing, and answering questions that net output barely improves for the first 1–2 weeks.

2. **They handle the wrong tickets**
   - If the backlog is mostly complex, technical, policy-sensitive, or emotionally charged, temps may not be able to resolve much without escalation.

3. **Quality drops**
   - Fast backlog-clearing can lead to incorrect answers, missed context, bad tone, policy violations, or promises your company cannot keep.

4. **Your permanent team gets interrupted more**
   - Instead of working the queue, senior support staff become trainers, reviewers, and escalation handlers.

5. **Customers get inconsistent answers**
   - Temps may interpret policies differently, especially around refunds, account issues, SLAs, or product limitations.

6. **Security and privacy risks**
   - Temporary staff may need access to customer data, internal tools, billing systems, or admin consoles. That creates risk if permissions are too broad or offboarding is sloppy.

7. **They reduce the visible backlog but not the actual problem**
   - Tickets may be closed quickly without truly resolving customer issues, leading to reopenings, follow-ups, escalations, or churn.

8. **Morale issues**
   - Existing staff may feel blamed, replaced, or burdened by having to train short-term people while still carrying the hard cases.

9. **No lasting capacity improvement**
   - After a month, the temps leave, and the queue starts growing again because the underlying drivers—bugs, confusing UX, poor docs, insufficient staffing, bad routing—remain.

10. **Backlog composition is misunderstood**
   - The backlog may include duplicates, stale tickets, spam, low-priority items, already-resolved issues, or tickets blocked on engineering. Hiring people won’t fix those.

11. **Metrics get distorted**
   - You may improve “tickets closed” while worsening customer satisfaction, first-contact resolution, escalation rate, or repeat contact rate.

12. **Knowledge disappears when they leave**
   - If temps learn useful patterns but don’t document them, the organization gains little after the month ends.

13. **They need tool/process changes you haven’t made**
   - Without macros, clear policies, tagging, routing rules, escalation paths, and decision trees, adding people just adds chaos.

A safer version of the plan:

- **Triage the backlog first**: separate simple, stale, complex, escalated, and engineering-blocked tickets.
- **Give temps narrow work**: password/account questions, known issue updates, duplicate cleanup, refund pre-checks, documentation links, simple follow-ups.
- **Create clear playbooks**: macros, decision trees, “when to escalate,” and examples of good responses.
- **Limit permissions**: minimum necessary access, with audit logs and strict offboarding.
- **Assign a reviewer/escalation lead**: one person responsible for QA and unblockers.
- **Track the right metrics**: reopen rate, CSAT, escalation rate, response accuracy, and backlog age—not just closure count.
- **Use the month to learn**: identify top contact drivers and fix the root causes.

The biggest risk is treating this as a staffing problem when it may actually be a process, product, documentation, or prioritization problem.
