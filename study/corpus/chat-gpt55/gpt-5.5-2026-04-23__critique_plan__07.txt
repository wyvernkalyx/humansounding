A 20% margin can be a **useful signal**, but it’s usually **not sufficient as the sole trigger** to open a second location.

The better trigger is: **the first location is sustainably profitable, operationally repeatable, and not dependent on the founder being there every day.**

I’d look at these conditions before expanding:

### 1. Margin is sustained, not temporary
A single month at 20% margin may not mean much. I’d want to see it for at least **3–6 consecutive months**, ideally through normal seasonality.

Clarify which margin you mean:

- **Gross margin**: not enough by itself.
- **Store-level EBITDA / operating margin**: much better.
- **Net profit after owner salary, rent, labor, marketing, and overhead allocation**: best.

If you mean **20% store-level operating margin**, that’s a strong sign. If you mean **20% gross margin**, that may be too weak.

### 2. The first location has proven unit economics
Before expanding, know:

- Average revenue per customer
- Customer acquisition cost
- Repeat purchase rate
- Labor as % of revenue
- Rent occupancy cost as % of revenue
- Break-even revenue
- Payback period on buildout/startup costs

A good expansion candidate usually has a clear answer to:  
**“If we spend $X opening location two, how long until we get that cash back?”**

Many operators target a **12–24 month payback**, depending on industry.

### 3. Location one can run without you
This is often the biggest issue.

If location one’s performance depends heavily on the founder, opening location two can damage both. Before expanding, you should have:

- A reliable manager in place
- Documented SOPs
- Hiring and training systems
- Inventory/vendor processes
- Reporting dashboards
- Quality control checks

A good test:  
**Can you step away from location one for 2–4 weeks and the business still hits targets?**

### 4. Demand exceeds current capacity or the market opportunity is proven
Open a second location because there is proven demand, not just because the first one is profitable.

Strong signs include:

- You’re turning customers away
- Wait times are too long
- Customers are traveling from the area where you’d open location two
- Marketing is profitable and scalable
- The brand has strong word-of-mouth/repeat behavior

### 5. You have enough cash cushion
Expansion often strains cash even when the second location is “successful.”

Before opening, ideally have:

- Full startup/buildout budget
- Working capital for ramp-up
- Reserve for delays/overruns
- Cash cushion so location one isn’t starved

A common mistake is funding the second location too aggressively from the first and weakening the original business.

### 6. You know why location one is working
This matters a lot. Is it the location? The manager? Your personal selling? Low rent? A unique local customer base?

Before replicating, isolate the drivers of success. Otherwise you may copy the wrong things.

---

## My recommendation

Use **20% margin as one gate**, not the trigger.

A better trigger would be:

> “We will open a second location when the first location has achieved at least 20% store-level operating margin for 3–6 months, has a manager who can run daily operations without founder involvement, has documented processes, produces positive cash flow after a fair owner salary, and the second location model shows a realistic payback within 12–24 months.”

That gives you a much safer expansion decision.

If you want a simple checklist, I’d use this:

| Expansion Criteria | Target |
|---|---:|
| Store-level operating margin | 20%+ for 3–6 months |
| Founder dependency | Low |
| Manager in place | Yes |
| SOPs documented | Yes |
| Cash reserves | 3–6 months operating cushion |
| Payback period on second location | 12–24 months preferred |
| Break-even revenue known | Yes |
| Demand in new market validated | Yes |

So: **20% margin is a good milestone, but not the right standalone trigger.** It should be paired with operational readiness, cash strength, and proof that the model can be repeated.
