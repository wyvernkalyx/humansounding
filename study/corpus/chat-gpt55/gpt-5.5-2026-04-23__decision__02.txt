In most cases:

- **Hire a second developer** if the work is **ongoing, core to the business, and requires product knowledge**.
- **Use contractors** if the work is **temporary, specialized, or has uncertain duration**.

My default recommendation would be: **hire a second developer if you expect at least 6–12 months of sustained engineering work.** Use contractors only to fill short-term gaps or handle specialized tasks.

## Choose a second developer if…

You have:

1. **A long backlog of core product work**
   - Features, infrastructure, bug fixes, internal tools, customer requests.
   - Work that will keep coming, not just a one-off project.

2. **Too much knowledge concentrated in one person**
   - A second developer reduces key-person risk.
   - They help with code review, architecture decisions, support, and continuity.

3. **You need faster iteration**
   - A full-time developer builds context over time.
   - They will usually become more productive than contractors on product work.

4. **Quality and maintainability matter**
   - Contractors may deliver quickly, but long-term ownership can suffer.
   - An employee has more incentive to keep the codebase healthy.

5. **Engineering is strategically important**
   - If software is central to the business, you generally want that capability in-house.

## Choose contractors if…

You need:

1. **Short-term capacity**
   - A deadline, migration, integration, or launch push.

2. **Specialized expertise**
   - Security review, DevOps, mobile app, data pipeline, UI redesign, performance tuning, etc.

3. **Flexibility**
   - If you are unsure whether the workload will continue, contractors reduce commitment.

4. **Immediate availability**
   - Hiring a good developer can take months.
   - Contractors can start faster if scoped well.

## Watch out for contractor overuse

Contractors can look cheaper or easier, but they often become expensive if they are doing ongoing product development.

Common issues:

- Repeated onboarding costs.
- Loss of product knowledge when they leave.
- Inconsistent quality.
- More management overhead.
- Less ownership of long-term architecture.
- Dependence on external people for core systems.

If contractors are doing the same type of work every month, that is usually a sign you should hire.

## Simple decision rule

Ask:

> “Will we still need this person’s work a year from now?”

If yes: **hire.**

If no or not sure: **use contractors.**

## Best practical option

A strong middle-ground approach:

1. **Hire a second developer for core product work.**
2. **Keep contractors for narrow, well-scoped projects.**

For example:

- Full-time developer: product features, bugs, architecture, support, code ownership.
- Contractors: design refresh, AWS setup, QA automation, analytics, one-off integrations.

## My recommendation

Unless your workload is clearly temporary, I would lean toward **hiring a second developer**.

A second in-house developer gives you compounding benefits: product knowledge, ownership, better continuity, less bottleneck risk, and stronger engineering capacity over time. Contractors are still useful, but mainly as a supplement — not as the backbone of your product development.
