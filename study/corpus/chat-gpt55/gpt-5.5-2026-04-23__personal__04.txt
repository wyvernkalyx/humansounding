A good goal is to make it about **safety and maintenance**, not blame. Here’s a low-conflict way to raise it:

### 1. Start friendly and specific
Pick a calm time—not right after a storm if emotions are high. Say something like:

> “Hey, I wanted to ask you about the tree along our property line. During the last few storms, branches have been coming down into my yard, and I’m worried someone could get hurt or something could be damaged. Would you be open to having an arborist take a look at it?”

This frames it as a shared concern, not an accusation.

### 2. Bring facts, not frustration
Mention dates/photos if you have them, but gently:

> “I took a couple photos just so we could show an arborist what’s been happening.”

Avoid “your tree is a hazard” unless a professional has said so.

### 3. Suggest a practical solution
Offer options:

- They hire an arborist to inspect it.
- You both get a neutral arborist’s opinion.
- If branches overhang your yard, ask whether they’d be comfortable with pruning.
- If cost is sensitive, you might offer to split the inspection or trimming if that feels worth preserving peace.

Example:

> “I’m not trying to make this a big thing. I just think it might be smart to have someone inspect it before the next storm season.”

### 4. Know your rights, but don’t lead with them
In many places, you can trim branches that overhang your property **up to the property line**, as long as you don’t damage or kill the tree—but laws vary. Don’t cut anything on their side or enter their property without permission.

If the tree is clearly diseased/dead and they know about it, liability can be different, but that’s very location-specific.

### 5. Put it in writing if needed
If the conversation goes nowhere, send a polite note or email:

> “Thanks for talking with me about the tree. I’m concerned because branches have fallen into my yard during recent storms. I’d appreciate it if you could have the tree inspected or trimmed. I’m happy to coordinate access from my side if helpful.”

This creates a record while staying cordial.

### 6. If it escalates, use neutral help
Before legal threats, consider:

- A certified arborist’s written assessment
- HOA/property manager, if applicable
- Local municipal code enforcement if it’s a dangerous tree
- Mediation services

### Key tone to aim for
“Can we solve this before it becomes a bigger problem?” rather than “You need to fix your tree.”

That approach gives your neighbor room to cooperate without feeling attacked.
