Here’s a practical playbook you can use—starting low-drama, but with a clear escalation path.

## 1. Start creating a paper trail
Before changing anything, make your contributions visible in writing.

- Send brief status updates: “This week I completed X, drafted Y, solved Z.”
- Recap decisions after meetings: “Following today’s discussion, I’ll continue refining the model I built for…”
- Keep drafts, timestamps, Slack/email messages, Jira tickets, commits, docs, etc.

This is not about being petty—it protects you and makes attribution easier.

## 2. Pre-empt credit theft before meetings
If you know your work may be discussed, put your name on it early.

Examples:
- “I’ll walk everyone through the analysis I completed on customer churn.”
- “I added the latest recommendations to the deck—happy to present that section.”
- “I’ve been leading the research on this, so I can give the background.”

If slides/docs are involved, include “Prepared by…” or clear owner names on sections.

## 3. Correct the record in the meeting—calmly and immediately
You don’t need to accuse them. Just re-anchor the work to yourself.

If they say, “I came up with this approach…,” you can say:

- “Yes, that builds on the framework I put together last week. I can add context on how I developed it.”
- “Thanks for highlighting that. When I ran the analysis, the key finding was…”
- “Just to clarify, I created the initial proposal, and I’d be happy to explain the reasoning.”
- “I’m glad this is useful. I led that piece, so I can answer questions.”

The tone matters: factual, calm, non-defensive.

## 4. Follow up after the meeting in writing
Send a recap that names ownership naturally.

Example:

> Thanks everyone. As discussed, I’ll continue developing the pricing analysis I presented, and Sam will handle the customer feedback section. Next steps are…

This helps correct attribution without creating a confrontation.

## 5. Talk to the colleague privately
If it happens more than once, address it directly.

Try:

> “I wanted to talk about something I’ve noticed. In the last couple of meetings, work I led was presented in a way that made it sound like it was yours. I’m sure that may not have been intentional, but I need my contributions to be accurately represented. Going forward, please refer to me as the owner of that work, or invite me to present it.”

Keep it specific. Mention examples. Avoid “you always” or “you’re stealing credit,” even if that’s how it feels.

## 6. If it continues, involve your manager
Don’t frame it as a personality conflict. Frame it as visibility, ownership, and career impact.

Say:

> “I’d like your guidance on something. I’ve noticed several instances where work I led was attributed to someone else in meetings. For example, X and Y. I’ve addressed it directly, but it’s continuing. I want to make sure ownership is clear because it affects performance evaluation and project accountability. How would you recommend we handle this?”

Bring documentation. Ask for a specific solution, like:
- You present your own work.
- Ownership is clarified in project plans.
- Your manager reinforces credit in meetings.
- Written updates include named contributors.

## 7. Build allies
If trusted teammates know you did the work, ask them to help reinforce attribution.

For example:

> “If this comes up in the meeting, could you help clarify that I led the analysis? I’m trying to make ownership more visible.”

Good colleagues will often say things like:

> “As [Your Name] found in their analysis…”

That can be powerful.

## 8. Be more visible generally
Sometimes credit theft works because the real contributor is too quiet. Increase your presence without bragging.

- Present your own sections.
- Speak early in meetings.
- Share concise updates with stakeholders.
- Ask to be included in meetings where your work is discussed.
- Put your name next to deliverables.

## 9. Know when to escalate harder
If the colleague is repeatedly misrepresenting your work after you’ve corrected them, that’s not a misunderstanding—it’s a pattern.

Escalate to:
- Your manager
- Their manager, if appropriate
- HR, if it affects reviews, promotion, compensation, or creates a hostile environment

Use facts, dates, and impact.

## The key principle
Don’t just complain after the fact. **Create visibility before, correct calmly during, document after, and escalate if repeated.**

A simple line you can use next time:

> “I’m glad this work is getting attention. Just to clarify, I led the analysis and developed the recommendation, so I can speak to the details.”
