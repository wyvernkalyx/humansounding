For an owner, the core difference is **what you legally own**:

- **Condo:** You own your individual unit as real estate, plus a shared interest in common areas.
- **Co-op:** You do **not** own your unit directly. You own shares in a corporation that owns the building, and those shares give you the right to occupy a specific apartment through a proprietary lease.

That legal difference affects almost everything.

## 1. Ownership

### Condo
You receive a deed to your unit. It is real property, like owning a house, just within a shared building.

### Co-op
You buy shares in the co-op corporation. The corporation owns the building. Your shares entitle you to live in a particular apartment.

**Practical meaning:** A condo is generally easier to understand, finance, sell, and rent out. A co-op is more like joining a collectively owned housing corporation.

## 2. Monthly costs

### Condo
You pay:
- Mortgage, if any
- Property taxes, usually billed directly to you
- Common charges/HOA fees for building maintenance and amenities

### Co-op
You pay:
- Your share loan, if any
- Monthly maintenance to the co-op corporation

Co-op maintenance often includes:
- Your share of building property taxes
- Building mortgage payments, if the co-op has an underlying mortgage
- Maintenance, staff, insurance, utilities, reserves, etc.

**Practical meaning:** Co-op monthly charges may look higher because they often bundle taxes and building debt.

## 3. Taxes

### Condo
You pay your own property tax bill.

### Co-op
The corporation pays the building’s property taxes, and your maintenance includes your allocated share. You may be able to deduct your portion of real estate taxes and mortgage interest, depending on tax rules and your situation.

## 4. Board approval

### Condo
Condo boards usually have limited power to reject a buyer. They may have a right of first refusal, but outright rejection is less common and harder.

### Co-op
Co-op boards often have broad approval rights. They can review your finances, interview you, and reject buyers, subject to fair housing laws.

**Practical meaning:** Buying or selling a co-op can be more restrictive and uncertain.

## 5. Financing

### Condo
You get a mortgage secured by your unit.

### Co-op
You get a share loan secured by your co-op shares and proprietary lease.

**Practical meaning:** Co-op financing can be more specialized. Lenders may scrutinize the building’s finances, owner-occupancy levels, reserves, and underlying mortgage.

## 6. Renting/subletting

### Condo
Usually more flexible, though the condo association may impose rules such as minimum lease terms or rental caps.

### Co-op
Often more restrictive. Many co-ops limit or prohibit subletting, require board approval, or allow subletting only after a certain period of ownership.

**Practical meaning:** If you may want to rent the apartment out later, a condo is usually better.

## 7. Renovations

### Condo
You need to follow building rules, but you generally have more autonomy inside your unit.

### Co-op
Renovations usually require board approval and may be more tightly controlled.

**Practical meaning:** Co-op living can involve more oversight.

## 8. Resale

### Condo
Typically easier to sell because there are fewer buyer restrictions and a larger financing market.

### Co-op
May be harder to sell because the buyer needs board approval, may need stronger financials, and may face stricter rules.

**Practical meaning:** Co-ops can be cheaper to buy, but less liquid.

## 9. Price

### Condo
Often more expensive than comparable co-ops.

### Co-op
Often cheaper on purchase price, especially in markets like New York City.

Why? Because co-ops usually come with more restrictions, more board control, and sometimes higher monthly maintenance.

## 10. Financial risk from the building

### Condo
You are responsible for your unit and your share of common expenses. The association can levy assessments if needed.

### Co-op
You are tied to the corporation’s financial health. If the co-op has a large underlying mortgage, poor reserves, or many nonpaying shareholders, that affects everyone.

**Practical meaning:** In a co-op, due diligence on the building’s finances is especially important.

---

## Simple summary

| Issue | Condo | Co-op |
|---|---|---|
| What you own | The unit itself | Shares in a corporation |
| Legal document | Deed | Stock certificate + proprietary lease |
| Monthly payment | Common charges + taxes | Maintenance, often including taxes |
| Board approval to buy | Usually limited | Often extensive |
| Renting out | Usually easier | Often restricted |
| Financing | Standard mortgage | Co-op/share loan |
| Resale | Usually easier | Can be harder |
| Purchase price | Usually higher | Often lower |
| Control by board | Moderate | Higher |

## Bottom line

If you want **flexibility, easier resale, and simpler ownership**, a **condo** is usually better.

If you want a **lower purchase price** and are comfortable with stricter building rules, board approval, and possible limits on renting or resale, a **co-op** can be a good option.
