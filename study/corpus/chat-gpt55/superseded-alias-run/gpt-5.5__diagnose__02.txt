Here’s a practical way to handle it without letting the conflict keep damaging the work.

## 1. Address it quickly and privately
Do not wait for it to “blow over” if performance is already suffering.

Meet with each person separately. Keep the tone calm and work-focused.

You can say:

> “I’ve noticed communication between you and [name] has broken down, and it’s affecting the work. I want to understand what’s going on and get us back to a professional working rhythm.”

Ask:
- What happened from their perspective?
- What do they need in order to work effectively?
- What are they willing to do differently?
- Are there any conduct, harassment, discrimination, or safety concerns?

Stay neutral. Do not let either meeting turn into a character assassination.

## 2. Separate personal feelings from work expectations
They do not need to like each other. They do need to communicate professionally.

Make that clear:

> “You don’t have to be friends, but you are expected to collaborate, respond to work-related messages, and avoid behavior that blocks the team.”

Define the minimum professional standard:
- Timely responses
- No ignoring essential requests
- No hostile comments or side-channel gossip
- Clear handoffs
- Escalation path if they disagree

## 3. Hold a mediated conversation
If it is appropriate and there are no serious misconduct issues, bring them together.

Set ground rules:
- One person speaks at a time
- Focus on work impact, not personalities
- No interrupting, blaming, or re-litigating every past incident
- The goal is a working agreement, not determining who is “right”

Structure the meeting like this:
1. “What is not working right now?”
2. “What does each of you need to do your job?”
3. “What communication rules will we use going forward?”
4. “What happens if there is another disagreement?”

Aim for a concrete agreement, for example:
- Use project management comments instead of informal messages
- Respond to work requests within one business day
- Weekly 15-minute sync with you present for a month
- Escalate blockers to you instead of going silent

## 4. Document the agreement
After the meeting, send a brief written summary:

> “To confirm, going forward you’ll communicate through [channel], respond within [timeframe], raise blockers by [method], and keep all interactions professional. We’ll review this in two weeks.”

This protects the work and avoids ambiguity.

## 5. Monitor behavior, not intentions
Watch whether the work improves:
- Are deadlines being met?
- Are handoffs happening?
- Are they communicating professionally?
- Are others being pulled into the conflict?

If one or both continue the behavior, treat it as a performance/conduct issue.

## 6. Escalate if necessary
Bring in HR, your manager, or a neutral mediator if:
- There are allegations of bullying, harassment, discrimination, retaliation, or threats
- One person refuses to cooperate
- The conflict is spreading across the team
- You are too close to the situation to be neutral
- Prior attempts have failed

## 7. Protect the team in the meantime
If work is at risk, temporarily adjust workflow:
- Clarify ownership
- Create written handoffs
- Route decisions through you
- Reduce unnecessary direct dependency between them

But avoid permanently designing around bad behavior unless absolutely necessary. The real issue still needs to be addressed.

## Bottom line
Act as the manager of the work, not the judge of the friendship. Your message should be:

> “I’m not asking you to be close. I am requiring you to work together professionally. The current situation is hurting the team, and it needs to change.”
