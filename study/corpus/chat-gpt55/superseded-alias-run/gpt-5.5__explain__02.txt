A **p-value** is a way to answer this question:

> “If nothing real was going on, how surprising would these results be?”

Imagine you flip a coin 10 times and get 9 heads.

You might wonder: “Is this coin unfair, or did I just get a weird result by chance?”

A p-value helps measure how unusual your result would be **assuming the coin is actually fair**.

- A **small p-value** means: “This result would be pretty unlikely if nothing unusual were happening.”
- A **large p-value** means: “This result could easily happen by random chance.”

For example:

- If the p-value is **0.50**, your result is not surprising at all.
- If the p-value is **0.01**, it means that results this extreme would happen about **1% of the time** if there were no real effect.

A common cutoff is **0.05**. If a study gets a p-value below 0.05, researchers often say the result is “statistically significant,” meaning it seems unlikely to be due to chance alone.

But important: a p-value **does not** tell you:

- the chance that the conclusion is true,
- how important the result is,
- or how big the effect is.

It only tells you how surprising the data would be **if there were no real effect**.
