Probably neither “full rebuild” nor “keep patching forever” should be the default. For a ten-year-old internal tool, the best answer is usually:

> **Keep it running, but start replacing the riskiest/highest-value parts incrementally.**

A full rebuild is tempting, but often more expensive and risky than expected. Continued patching is cheaper short-term, but can become a slow tax on productivity, reliability, and security.

## When you should keep patching

Keep patching if most of these are true:

- The tool is stable and rarely causes incidents.
- Users are mostly satisfied.
- The business process it supports is not changing much.
- The codebase is understandable enough for current developers.
- Security/compliance risk is low.
- Hosting/runtime dependencies are still supported.
- Enhancement requests are occasional and not painful.
- The cost of rebuilding would outweigh the benefit.

In that case, you may only need targeted maintenance: dependency upgrades, documentation, test coverage, UI cleanup, and small architectural fixes.

## When you should rebuild or replace

A larger rebuild becomes more justified if several of these are true:

- Every change takes too long or breaks something unrelated.
- Only one or two people understand the system.
- The technology stack is obsolete or unsupported.
- Security, compliance, or auditability is a growing concern.
- The tool blocks process improvements or automation.
- Data quality is poor and getting worse.
- Users have built spreadsheets or workarounds because the tool no longer fits.
- Operating costs are high compared with the value delivered.
- You are planning significant business process changes anyway.

If the tool is slowing the organization down or creating material risk, patching may be false economy.

## Avoid the “big bang rewrite” if possible

A complete rebuild from scratch is risky because:

- It takes longer than expected.
- Legacy behavior is often undocumented.
- Users may depend on quirks nobody remembers.
- New systems frequently miss edge cases.
- The old system must still be maintained during the rebuild.

A safer approach is usually the **strangler pattern**:

1. Stabilize the current tool.
2. Identify the most painful modules or workflows.
3. Build replacements one piece at a time.
4. Migrate users and data gradually.
5. Retire old parts as new ones prove themselves.

This gives you value earlier and reduces the chance of a failed rewrite.

## A practical decision framework

Score the current tool from 1–5 in these areas:

| Area | Question |
|---|---|
| Business fit | Does it still support how the business works? |
| Maintainability | Can developers safely change it? |
| Reliability | Does it cause downtime or operational issues? |
| Security/compliance | Is it safe and auditable enough? |
| User experience | Are users productive and satisfied? |
| Integration | Does it connect cleanly with other systems? |
| Cost | Is the cost of maintenance acceptable? |
| Strategic importance | Is this tool important to future operations? |

If most scores are healthy, patch and modernize selectively.

If maintainability, security, or business fit are poor, plan replacement.

If the system is important but fragile, do incremental modernization rather than a full rewrite.

## My default recommendation

For a ten-year-old internal tool, I would usually recommend:

1. **Do not start with a full rebuild.**
2. **Run a short discovery phase** — 2 to 4 weeks.
3. **Map current workflows, pain points, risks, and hidden dependencies.**
4. **Estimate the cost of three options:**
   - Maintain and patch
   - Incremental modernization
   - Full rebuild or replacement
5. **Pick the lowest-risk path that removes the biggest constraints.**

In many cases, the winning option is:

> **Stabilize the legacy tool now, then incrementally replace the parts that are costly, risky, or strategically limiting.**

## Key questions to answer before deciding

- How many users depend on it?
- How often does it break?
- How long does a typical change take?
- Is the technology stack still supported?
- Are there security or compliance concerns?
- How much institutional knowledge is trapped in the tool?
- Are users asking for major workflow changes?
- Could an off-the-shelf tool replace it?
- What is the cost of doing nothing for another two years?
- What would the organization gain from a modern version?

If you can answer those clearly, the decision will likely become obvious.
