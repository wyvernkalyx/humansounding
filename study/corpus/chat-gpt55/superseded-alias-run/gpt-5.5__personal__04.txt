Here’s a low-conflict way to bring it up:

1. **Pick a calm time — not right after a storm.**  
   Approach when you’re both relaxed, ideally in person.

2. **Lead with safety, not blame.**  
   Say something like:  
   *“Hey, I wanted to mention something before it becomes a bigger problem. During the last few storms, branches from your tree have been falling into my yard. I’m a little concerned about safety/property damage. Would you be open to having an arborist take a look?”*

3. **Make it collaborative.**  
   Instead of “your tree is a problem,” frame it as:  
   *“I’d like to figure out the best way to handle it so neither of us has a bigger issue later.”*

4. **Offer a practical next step.**  
   You could suggest getting an arborist’s opinion, and if appropriate:  
   *“If trimming on my side would help, I’m happy to coordinate so it’s done properly.”*

5. **Document quietly.**  
   Take photos of fallen branches and dates after storms. Don’t wave them around as evidence at first—just keep them in case the issue escalates or insurance gets involved.

6. **Know the general rules, but don’t open with legal threats.**  
   In many places, you can trim branches that overhang your property line, but you usually can’t trespass or harm the tree. Tree liability rules vary a lot by location, especially if the tree is visibly diseased/dead, so local advice may matter.

7. **If they ignore a clear hazard, put it in writing politely.**  
   Example:  
   *“Just following up on our conversation. I’m concerned that branches from the tree continue to fall during storms. I’d appreciate it if you could have someone assess it when you’re able.”*

The key is to make it about **preventing a problem together**, not accusing them of neglect. Most neighbors respond better when they feel invited to solve something rather than blamed.
