Compound interest means you earn interest not only on your original money, but also on the interest that has already been added.

For example, suppose you invest $1,000 at 10% interest per year.

- After year 1: you earn $100, so you have $1,100.
- After year 2: you earn 10% of $1,100, which is $110, so you have $1,210.
- After year 3: you earn 10% of $1,210, which is $121, so you have $1,331.

The key idea is that the amount earning interest keeps growing. This creates a “snowball effect.”

A common formula is:

\[
A = P(1+r)^t
\]

where:

- \(A\) is the final amount
- \(P\) is the starting amount
- \(r\) is the interest rate per period
- \(t\) is the number of periods

So if you invest $1,000 at 10% for 30 years:

\[
1000(1.10)^{30} \approx 17,449
\]

That means $1,000 grows to about $17,449 without adding any extra money.

Compound interest surprises people because humans tend to think linearly. We expect steady growth, like “10% per year means it grows by about the same amount each year.” But compounding is exponential: the growth starts slowly, then accelerates dramatically over time.

It can work for you with savings and investing, but it can also work against you with debt. Credit card debt, for example, can grow quickly because interest is added to the balance, and then future interest is charged on that larger balance.
