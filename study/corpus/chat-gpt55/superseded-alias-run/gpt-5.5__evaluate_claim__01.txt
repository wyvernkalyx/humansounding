“Never” is too strong. Paying off a low-interest mortgage early can be either a bad or good choice depending on your finances, goals, and risk tolerance.

### Why it often *doesn’t* make sense to pay it off early
If your mortgage rate is low, the “return” from extra payments is basically the interest rate you avoid. For example, paying extra on a 3% mortgage is like earning a guaranteed 3% after-tax/adjusted return. If you could reasonably earn more elsewhere—retirement accounts, diversified investments, paying off higher-interest debt—then extra mortgage payments may be less attractive.

Other reasons not to rush:
- **Liquidity:** Money put into home equity is harder to access than cash or investments.
- **Retirement savings:** If you’re not maxing tax-advantaged accounts, those may be a higher priority.
- **Inflation:** A fixed low-rate mortgage becomes easier to pay over time if income/prices rise.
- **Tax considerations:** Mortgage interest deductions may matter for some people, though many don’t benefit due to the standard deduction.
- **Prepayment penalties:** Rare today, but worth checking.

### Why paying it off early *can* make sense
Even with a low rate, early payoff may be reasonable if:
- You already have a strong emergency fund.
- You’re on track with retirement savings.
- You have no high-interest debt.
- You value lower monthly obligations and peace of mind.
- You’re nearing retirement and want reduced fixed expenses.
- You prefer a guaranteed “return” over market risk.

### A simple priority order
Usually, before making extra mortgage payments, consider:
1. Pay off credit cards/high-interest debt.
2. Build an emergency fund.
3. Contribute enough to get employer retirement match.
4. Fund retirement/HSAs/other tax-advantaged accounts.
5. Then compare extra mortgage payments vs taxable investing or other goals.

### Bottom line
If your mortgage rate is very low—say 2–4%—it’s often mathematically better to invest extra money rather than pay the mortgage early. But if becoming debt-free improves your security or peace of mind, paying it down can still be a valid choice. The best answer depends on your rate, tax situation, investment options, cash reserves, and goals.
