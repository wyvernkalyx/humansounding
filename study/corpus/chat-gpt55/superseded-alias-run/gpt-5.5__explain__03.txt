Software estimates are “always wrong” because they are predictions about an uncertain future, not measurements of a known quantity.

Key reasons:

1. **Software work is discovery-heavy**  
   Unlike manufacturing, where you repeat a known process, software often involves figuring out what to build while building it. Unknown technical problems, unclear requirements, and design tradeoffs appear along the way.

2. **Requirements change**  
   Stakeholders learn more once they see partial versions of the product. Priorities shift, edge cases appear, and “small changes” can affect architecture, testing, UX, data models, or integrations.

3. **Complexity is hard to see upfront**  
   A task may sound simple: “add login,” “export to PDF,” “support multiple currencies.” But hidden complexity may include security, permissions, error handling, performance, compliance, localization, and legacy system behavior.

4. **Dependencies create uncertainty**  
   Estimates often depend on APIs, third-party services, other teams, infrastructure, approvals, libraries, deployment pipelines, or data quality. Any delay or surprise in those areas affects the estimate.

5. **People are not interchangeable machines**  
   Developer experience, context switching, interruptions, illness, meetings, onboarding, codebase familiarity, and team communication all change delivery speed.

6. **Optimism bias is common**  
   Developers and managers tend to imagine the happy path: the code works, the requirement is clear, tests pass, and nothing breaks. Real projects include debugging, rework, misunderstandings, and integration problems.

7. **The estimate is often for coding, but the work is more than coding**  
   Real delivery includes analysis, design, reviews, testing, documentation, deployment, monitoring, bug fixes, and stakeholder feedback. These are easy to undercount.

8. **Precision is mistaken for accuracy**  
   Saying “this will take 17 days” sounds scientific, but it may be less honest than saying “probably 2–4 weeks.” Software estimates are usually better expressed as ranges with uncertainty.

So estimates are not useless; they are just imperfect. A good estimate communicates uncertainty, assumptions, risks, and confidence level. The problem happens when an estimate is treated as a guarantee.
