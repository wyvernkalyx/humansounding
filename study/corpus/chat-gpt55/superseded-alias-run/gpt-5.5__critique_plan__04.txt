A lot *can* go right — extra capacity can help — but the common failure modes are:

### 1. Onboarding takes longer than expected
If your support workflows, product knowledge, macros, escalation paths, and tooling are complex, the temps may spend a meaningful chunk of the month ramping instead of clearing tickets.

**Risk:** permanent team loses time training them, worsening the backlog short-term.

---

### 2. They handle only the easiest tickets
Temps may be able to close simple, repetitive cases, but the backlog may be dominated by messy, technical, angry, or account-specific issues.

**Risk:** ticket count drops superficially while the hardest, oldest, highest-risk cases remain.

---

### 3. Quality and consistency may suffer
If they rush or lack context, they may give incorrect answers, miss edge cases, over-refund, under-escalate, or create inconsistent customer experiences.

**Risk:** reopened tickets, escalations, churn, or brand damage.

---

### 4. Security and permissions issues
Support staff often need access to customer data, admin tools, billing systems, internal docs, or production-adjacent systems.

**Risk:** overbroad access, data mishandling, compliance problems, or cleanup issues when they leave.

---

### 5. The backlog may be a symptom, not the problem
If the backlog exists because of product bugs, unclear docs, poor routing, bad automation, understaffing, or a recent launch issue, temps only treat the pile — not the inflow.

**Risk:** backlog returns as soon as the temps leave.

---

### 6. Permanent staff may get distracted
Your experienced team may need to review temp responses, answer questions, triage what they can handle, and fix mistakes.

**Risk:** your best people become supervisors instead of solving the hardest problems.

---

### 7. No clear definition of success
“Fix the backlog” can mean many things: reduce ticket count, reduce first-response time, close tickets older than 14 days, handle specific categories, improve CSAT, etc.

**Risk:** after a month, everyone disagrees on whether it worked.

---

### 8. Customer context gets lost
Temps may not understand which customers are strategic, at risk, high-value, regulated, or already frustrated.

**Risk:** mishandling sensitive accounts or missing retention opportunities.

---

### 9. Temporary workers may lack accountability after exit
If they make poor notes, apply one-off fixes, or leave tickets half-resolved, your team inherits the mess.

**Risk:** hidden cleanup cost after the month ends.

---

### 10. Morale impact
Existing support staff may feel the company is applying a band-aid instead of addressing workload, tooling, compensation, or staffing issues.

**Risk:** burnout continues, and attrition risk increases.

---

## How to make it work better

If you do hire them, constrain the scope:

- Give temps a narrow queue: password resets, billing questions, documentation links, simple refunds, known issue responses, etc.
- Create clear macros and decision trees.
- Require escalation rules for anything risky, angry, technical, legal, or high-value.
- Limit permissions.
- Assign one internal owner for QA and coaching.
- Track reopened tickets, CSAT, handle time, and escalations — not just closures.
- Use the month to identify root causes of ticket volume.
- Have a cleanup/offboarding plan before they start.

The biggest danger is assuming “two people for one month” equals “two months of productive support capacity.” In practice, it may be much less unless the work is well-scoped and the system around them is ready.
