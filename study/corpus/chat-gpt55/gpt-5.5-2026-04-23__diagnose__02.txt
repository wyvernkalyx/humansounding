Here’s a practical way to handle it before it becomes a bigger team problem.

## 1. Address it quickly, but calmly
Do not let the silence become the new normal. If the work is suffering, it is now a management issue, not just a personal disagreement.

You can say something like:

> “I’ve noticed communication between you two has broken down, and it’s affecting the work. I’m not here to assign blame, but we need to resolve the working relationship enough for the team to function.”

## 2. Speak to each person privately first
Meet one-on-one with each team member. Your goal is to understand, not judge.

Ask:
- “What happened from your perspective?”
- “What impact is this having on your work?”
- “What do you need in order to work productively with them?”
- “Is there anything I need to know that involves disrespect, harassment, or a serious conduct issue?”

Listen for whether this is:
- A misunderstanding
- A personality clash
- A workload/role conflict
- A performance issue
- Something more serious, like bullying or harassment

If it involves misconduct, discrimination, threats, or harassment, involve HR or the appropriate escalation process immediately.

## 3. Make the expectation clear
Even if they dislike each other, they still need to communicate professionally.

You can be direct:

> “You do not have to be friends. But you do have to communicate respectfully, share necessary information, and meet work commitments.”

Avoid making the goal “reconcile personally.” Make the goal “work effectively.”

## 4. Bring them together for a structured conversation
If it is appropriate and safe, hold a mediated meeting. Keep it focused on work.

Use a structure like:

1. Each person explains the work impact without interruption.
2. Each person states what they need going forward.
3. Agree on specific behaviors and communication norms.
4. Confirm next steps and deadlines.

Keep the conversation away from character judgments like “You’re rude” or “You never listen.” Redirect toward observable behavior:

> “Let’s focus on specific examples and what needs to happen differently.”

## 5. Create a working agreement
Be concrete. For example:

- They will respond to project-related messages within one business day.
- They will use the project channel for key updates.
- They will copy you or the project lead on critical handoffs for the next few weeks.
- They will raise blockers directly instead of avoiding each other.
- Disagreements will be escalated after one good-faith attempt to resolve them.

Put the agreement in writing, even if briefly.

## 6. Monitor the work, not the drama
Follow up after a week or two.

Ask:
- “Are handoffs happening?”
- “Are decisions getting made?”
- “Is communication professional?”
- “Is the project back on track?”

If behavior improves, acknowledge it. If it does not, move into performance management territory.

## 7. Do not let one person weaponize silence
If one or both continue refusing to communicate, make the consequence clear:

> “Refusing to communicate about required work is not acceptable. If this continues, we’ll need to address it as a performance issue.”

## 8. Consider whether the team structure needs changing
Sometimes the conflict reveals a deeper issue:
- Unclear ownership
- Competing priorities
- Ambiguous decision rights
- Poor handoff process
- One person repeatedly behaving badly

Fixing the interpersonal tension may not be enough if the system is creating the conflict.

## Simple immediate plan
1. Meet each person privately today or tomorrow.
2. Clarify that professional communication is required.
3. Hold a mediated conversation if appropriate.
4. Create a short working agreement.
5. Follow up within one week.
6. Escalate to HR or formal performance management if they refuse or if misconduct is involved.

The key is not to force friendship. The key is to restore professional behavior and protect the work.
