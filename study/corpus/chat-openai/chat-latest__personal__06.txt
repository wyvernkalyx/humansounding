Don’t turn it into a fight over who deserves credit; make your ownership visible and create a record of it.

- In the meeting, correct the record calmly and immediately: “Yes, that’s the approach I developed from the customer data last week. I can walk everyone through the reasoning and next steps.” This claims ownership without accusing anyone.
- Afterward, speak to the colleague privately: “I’ve noticed a few times that work I developed has been presented without mentioning my contribution. Going forward, when you present my work, I need you to attribute it to me—or bring me in to present it.” Keep it specific and focused on the behavior you want changed.
- Make attribution harder to erase. Send progress updates yourself, put your name on documents/decks, circulate meeting notes, and use shared channels: “Here’s the analysis I completed and the recommendations I’m proposing.” Include collaborators and their contributions fairly.
- If it continues, document concrete incidents—date, project, what you produced, and what happened—and raise the pattern with your manager. Frame it as an ownership/workflow problem rather than a character judgment: “I’ve addressed this directly with Alex, but it has continued. Here are three examples. I’d like clearer ownership and presentation responsibilities.”
- If your manager dismisses a repeated, well-documented pattern, that tells you something important about the organization. Depending on the stakes, the next step may be a skip-level manager or HR, especially if it affects performance evaluations, promotions, compensation, or involves retaliation.

One thing to avoid is waiting until you’re furious and then making a broad accusation like “You always steal my ideas.” Specific examples plus a specific request are much more difficult to deflect.

If this is happening in your next meeting, a useful sentence to have ready is: “I’d like to add some context since I led this piece of the work.” Then take the floor.
