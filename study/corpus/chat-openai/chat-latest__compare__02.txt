For someone who expects to move every four years, renting is often financially safer, though buying can still win in favorable markets.

- Transaction costs: Buying and selling is expensive. Closing costs when buying can run roughly 2–5% of the purchase price, while selling costs can consume another several percent. Over only four years, these costs can outweigh the equity you build.
- Equity: Early mortgage payments are interest-heavy, so you generally build principal slowly at first. Appreciation can make buying worthwhile, but four years is a relatively short period to rely on home-price gains.
- Renting: Moving is easier, upfront costs are lower, and the landlord generally bears major repair and maintenance costs. This fits a frequent-mover lifestyle well.
- Buying: You gain potential appreciation, some principal repayment, control over the property, and protection from rent increases while you own it. But you also take on maintenance, property taxes, insurance, and the risk that prices are down when you need to sell.
- Flexibility: With a predictable four-year move cycle, renting avoids the possibility of delaying a move or selling at a bad time.

A useful rule of thumb is that buying becomes more attractive the longer you stay; four years is near or below the break-even period in many markets. Don't decide based simply on whether the mortgage payment is lower than rent. Compare the unrecoverable costs of each option—rent versus mortgage interest, taxes, insurance, maintenance, transaction costs, and the opportunity cost of your down payment.
