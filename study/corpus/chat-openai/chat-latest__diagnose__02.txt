Treat this as a work problem you need to manage, not a friendship you need to repair. They don't have to like each other, but they do need to communicate well enough to do their jobs.

- First, speak with each person privately. Describe the specific impact you've observed—missed handoffs, delays, errors, meetings being avoided—rather than diagnosing their relationship. Ask what is preventing effective collaboration and what they need to work productively.
- Set a clear expectation: professional, timely communication is part of the job. They can disagree or limit unnecessary interaction, but refusing necessary communication isn't a workable arrangement.
- Then bring them together if it's appropriate and safe. Keep the conversation focused on behaviors and workflow rather than deciding who was "right." Agree on concrete rules: which channel they'll use, response times, who owns what, how decisions are recorded, and how disagreements get escalated.
- Follow up soon, with measurable expectations. For example: "For the next two weeks, all handoffs go through the project tracker, questions get acknowledged within one business day, and unresolved issues come to me after one attempt to resolve them directly."
- Document the expectations and check progress. If either person continues refusing required collaboration, address that as a performance or conduct issue under your normal processes.

Don't force an apology, demand that they become friendly, or run an improvised mediation if there are allegations involving harassment, discrimination, threats, retaliation, or another serious policy issue. In those cases, involve HR or the appropriate internal process rather than trying to reconcile them yourself.

A useful opening in each 1:1 is: "I've noticed communication between you and [name] has largely stopped, and it's affecting X and Y. I don't need you two to be friends, but I do need the work relationship to function. I'd like to understand what's getting in the way and agree on what needs to change."
