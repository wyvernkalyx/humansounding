No. “Never pay off a low-interest mortgage early” is too absolute. It’s a tradeoff between a guaranteed return, liquidity, taxes, risk, and personal preferences.

Paying extra principal effectively gives you a guaranteed return roughly equal to the mortgage interest you avoid. So paying down a 3% fixed mortgage is economically similar to earning a low-risk ~3% return, adjusted for any mortgage-interest tax benefit you actually receive.

Keeping the mortgage can make more sense when you can put the money somewhere more valuable. For example:

- If you have a 3% mortgage but can earn more than 3% after taxes in a genuinely low-risk investment, keeping the mortgage can come out ahead financially.
- Long-term stock investments have historically offered higher expected returns than a low mortgage rate, but that return is uncertain. Paying down the mortgage is guaranteed.
- Contributions that receive an employer retirement-plan match generally deserve priority over extra mortgage payments.
- Paying off higher-interest debt first is usually more valuable.
- Maintaining an adequate emergency fund matters because money used to pay down a mortgage becomes much less liquid.

Taxes can complicate the comparison. A mortgage deduction only helps if you actually benefit from itemizing, while interest or investment earnings may be taxable. So you should compare after-tax numbers rather than simply comparing the mortgage rate with an advertised investment return.

There are also perfectly reasonable non-mathematical reasons to pay it off: reducing required monthly expenses, approaching retirement without a house payment, or simply preferring a guaranteed reduction in debt over taking investment risk.

As a rough example, I generally wouldn’t be in a hurry to prepay a fixed 2.5% mortgage while neglecting retirement savings. I’d view a 7% mortgage quite differently. Rates in between require more judgment.
