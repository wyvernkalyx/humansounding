“Uber but for dog walking” is useful shorthand, but weak as a pitch because it explains the analogy more than the business.

It leaves several important questions unanswered:

- What specifically is Uber-like: instant booking, location matching, pricing, ratings, or independent contractors?
- Why does dog walking need an on-demand model? Many owners need recurring, trusted care rather than the nearest available walker.
- Trust matters much more when someone is handling your pet and potentially entering your home. The phrase can accidentally suggest walkers are interchangeable.
- It says nothing about your differentiation. Companies such as Rover and Wag already offer app-based dog walking, so listeners may hear “an existing product with an Uber interface.”
- It describes mechanics, not customer value. The stronger pitch says who has the problem, what problem you solve, and why your approach is better.

As a conversational opener, the analogy can still work: “We make it as easy to book a vetted dog walker as it is to book a ride, with recurring scheduling and live walk tracking.” Then add whatever makes your product meaningfully different.

A useful test is: after hearing the pitch, can someone explain why a dog owner would choose you over Rover, Wag, or a trusted local walker? If not, the pitch needs another sentence.
