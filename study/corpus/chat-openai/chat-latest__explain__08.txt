At the federal level, a bill generally becomes U.S. law through these steps:

1. Introduction: A member of Congress introduces a bill in either the House of Representatives or the Senate. Revenue bills must originate in the House.

2. Committee review: The bill is referred to one or more committees, which may hold hearings, amend it, approve it, or decline to advance it.

3. Floor action: If advanced, the bill is debated and voted on by the chamber where it was introduced. If it passes, it goes to the other chamber, where it undergoes a similar committee and voting process.

4. Resolving differences: The House and Senate must ultimately pass the same text. If they pass different versions, they can negotiate a compromise—sometimes through a conference committee—and both chambers must approve the final version.

5. Presidential action: The enrolled bill is sent to the president. The president can sign it, making it law, or veto it and return it to Congress.

6. Possible veto override: Congress can override a presidential veto with a two-thirds vote in both the House and Senate, making the bill law despite the veto.

There are also timing rules. If the president takes no action for 10 days (excluding Sundays) while Congress remains in session, the bill becomes law without a signature. If Congress adjourns in a way that prevents the bill's return during that period, the bill does not become law; this is known as a pocket veto.

This is the standard federal process, though congressional procedures can make the path considerably more complicated in practice.
