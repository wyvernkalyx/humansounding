It can work as a short-term backlog burn-down, but a one-month temp engagement has several common failure modes:

- Ramp-up eats the month. If your support domain, tools, or policies are complicated, temps may become productive only near the end.
- Permanent staff become trainers. Your strongest agents can lose ticket-solving time to onboarding, supervision, and reviewing temp responses, so net capacity is lower than “two extra people.”
- Quality drops under backlog pressure. New hires may close tickets quickly but incorrectly, creating reopenings, escalations, refunds, or unhappy customers.
- You clear symptoms rather than inflow. If tickets are accumulating because of a product defect, bad self-service, routing problems, or insufficient ongoing staffing, the backlog returns after the temps leave.
- The oldest tickets may be unusually difficult. A backlog isn't necessarily interchangeable work; easy cases often get handled first, leaving a concentrated pool of complicated cases that newcomers are least equipped to solve.
- Access and security create overhead. Provisioning customer-data access for short-term workers, then reliably removing it, can involve privacy, compliance, and audit requirements.
- Knowledge disappears after four weeks. Anything the temps learn about recurring customer problems is lost unless you deliberately capture it.
- Metrics can fool you. “Tickets closed” may improve while response quality, reopen rate, customer satisfaction, or the number of new tickets waiting gets worse.

Before hiring, I'd estimate the economics as: backlog ÷ expected net resolutions per temp after training, while accounting for the permanent-team hours required to support them. Give temps well-defined, lower-complexity ticket categories, canned-but-reviewed guidance, clear escalation rules, and limited permissions.

Most importantly, pair the temporary capacity with a diagnosis of why the backlog exists. If it was caused by a one-off spike, two temps may be exactly the right tool. If incoming volume consistently exceeds your team's capacity, you're buying a one-month delay rather than fixing the backlog.
