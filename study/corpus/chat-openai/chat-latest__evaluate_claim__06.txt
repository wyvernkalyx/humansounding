Partly, but the popular claim oversimplifies the history.

The idea that breakfast is especially important predates modern cereal companies by centuries. Doctors and nutrition writers were recommending breakfast long before corn flakes existed, although attitudes toward breakfast varied considerably by time and place.

What cereal companies did do was aggressively promote breakfast—and breakfast cereal in particular. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, companies such as Kellogg's helped turn packaged cereal into a standard American breakfast. Later advertising reinforced messages about breakfast's nutritional importance. The famous phrase "breakfast is the most important meal of the day" is often attributed online to a Kellogg's marketing campaign, but there isn't good evidence that Kellogg's actually invented the phrase; versions of the sentiment appear independently in earlier health and nutrition writing.

As for the nutrition claim itself, modern evidence doesn't establish that everyone needs breakfast or that it's inherently the day's "most important" meal. Eating breakfast can be useful for some people—children, athletes, people who are hungry in the morning, or those who find it helps their concentration and eating patterns—but routinely skipping it isn't automatically unhealthy. Overall diet quality, energy intake, and individual circumstances matter more than assigning one meal universal importance.

So: cereal companies helped popularize and commercially exploit the idea, but they didn't simply invent breakfast's supposed importance from scratch.
