The biggest difference is what you legally own and how much control the building has over you.

- Condo: You own your individual unit as real estate, plus a shared interest in common areas. You receive a deed and pay property taxes on your unit. Condos are generally easier to finance, rent out, renovate, and sell, although association rules still apply.
- Co-op: You don't own your apartment itself. You own shares in a corporation that owns the entire building, and those shares give you the right to occupy a particular apartment under a proprietary lease. Property taxes and often the building's underlying mortgage are generally paid by the corporation and passed through through your monthly maintenance.

For an owner, the practical differences are significant:

- Buying/selling: Co-op boards often require extensive financial disclosures and can approve or reject buyers. Condo boards generally have much less ability to block a sale.
- Financing: Condo mortgages work much like mortgages on other real estate. Co-op financing is technically a loan secured by your shares and proprietary lease, and fewer lenders offer it.
- Monthly charges: Condo owners pay common charges/HOA fees and usually pay their own property taxes separately. Co-op owners pay maintenance, which commonly includes their share of property taxes and may include utilities and the building's mortgage payments.
- Renting: Condos are usually more permissive. Co-ops frequently restrict subletting or impose additional requirements and fees.
- Renovations: Both can regulate construction, but co-op boards often exercise tighter control.
- Governance: Both have boards and rules, but a co-op corporation generally has broader control over shareholders and occupancy.
- Taxes and appreciation: Both can appreciate, but condos tend to have a larger buyer/investor pool and often command higher prices in markets where both exist. Co-op shareholders can generally deduct their allocated share of qualifying property taxes and mortgage interest, subject to applicable tax rules.

A useful shorthand is: a condo is "I own this apartment"; a co-op is "I own part of the company that owns this building, and that ownership lets me live in this apartment."

If you're choosing between two actual properties, the co-op's financial statements, underlying building mortgage, sublet policy, and board requirements are especially important—not just the purchase price and monthly maintenance.
