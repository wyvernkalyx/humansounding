For a first-time investor, broad index funds are usually the simpler starting point. Individual stocks can offer higher upside, but they require more research and expose you to much more company-specific risk.

- Index funds: A single fund can hold hundreds or thousands of companies. This gives you instant diversification, typically at low cost. You don't need to predict which companies will succeed, and broad funds such as total-market or S&P 500 index funds have historically been effective for long-term investing. They can still fall substantially when the overall market declines.
- Individual stocks: You choose specific companies. If you pick a major winner, you can outperform the market, but a company can also permanently lose much or all of its value. Building a diversified stock portfolio takes more money, research, discipline, and ongoing attention.
- Time and complexity: Index investing can be largely automated. Individual-stock investing involves evaluating businesses, valuations, financial statements, and changing conditions—and even professionals often fail to beat broad indexes consistently over long periods.
- Costs and taxes: Both can be inexpensive with modern brokers, but frequent stock trading can increase transaction-related costs and taxable gains. Low-turnover index funds tend to be relatively tax-efficient.

For many beginners with a long time horizon, a low-cost, broad-market index fund is a reasonable core investment. Someone interested in stock picking could keep most of their portfolio diversified and use a smaller portion for individual stocks.

Before investing, it generally makes sense to have an emergency fund and address high-interest debt. The right mix also depends on when you'll need the money and how much volatility you can tolerate.
