A few things to sort out before you reply:

**Decide whether you're saying no to *this* business or to *investing in friends' businesses.*** The second is a much easier thing to say, and for most people it's also true. Early-stage private investments are illiquid, most fail, and mixing it with a friendship means you can lose the money and the relationship. You don't have to argue that her plan is flawed to decline — you can decline on principle.

**Answer quickly.** Letting it hang for two weeks reads as either avoidance or a maybe, and it makes the eventual no land harder.

**Figure out if she actually wants feedback.** Many people asking for money are not asking for a critique. If she wants your honest read, give it once — specifically, not vaguely — and then drop it. Repeating your concerns after she's heard them isn't helping, it's lobbying.

Some language that tends to work:

> "I really appreciate you thinking of me, and I'm genuinely excited for you. But I have a rule about not investing in private businesses — it's not liquid and I'm not in a position to tie money up like that. I'd rather be your friend through this than your investor."

Or if you want to leave the door open on honesty:

> "I'm going to pass on investing — that's a firm no, and it's about my own finances more than your plan. If you ever want me to be a sounding board or poke holes in something, I'm happy to do that, but only if you want it."

**What to avoid:**

- "Let me think about it" when you already know the answer.
- Softening it into a maybe, which invites a follow-up pitch.
- Offering a long list of reasons her business will fail as justification for your no. That turns a financial decision into a referendum on her judgment, and she'll defend the plan instead of hearing you.
- Investing an amount that would upset you to lose. If you do want to give something as a gesture of support, treat it mentally as a gift, say so out loud, and never expect it back.

**Offer something real if you can.** Introductions, a few hours of help, being a customer, spreading the word. It's not a consolation prize — it makes clear that the no is about money, not about her.

If she takes it badly, that's information about the friendship, but give her a little room — people who are raising money are usually stressed and hearing a lot of nos. It often blows over.
