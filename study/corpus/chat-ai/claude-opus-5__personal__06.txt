A few things actually work, and they depend on what's happening.

**First, figure out which version this is**

- They present joint work as solely theirs
- They restate your idea ten minutes later and it lands as theirs
- They report your work upward without you in the room
- Ownership was genuinely murkier than you remember

The last one is worth thirty seconds of honest reflection, not because you're probably wrong, but because if you escalate on shaky ground you lose credibility for the real instances.

**In the moment**

Don't accuse. Just insert yourself back into the narrative, calmly and specifically:

- *"Right — that's the approach I laid out Tuesday. The part I'd add is..."* Then keep talking. You've reclaimed it and moved on before anyone feels awkward.
- Ask a question only the author could answer: *"Glad that's landing. When I was working through it, the messy part was the data lag — how were you thinking about handling that?"* If they didn't do the work, this is quietly devastating and you didn't accuse anyone of anything.
- If you have an ally in the room, ask them privately to amplify: *"I want to come back to what she said earlier..."* This works better than self-advocacy because it comes from outside.

**Structurally, so it stops happening**

This matters more than any in-meeting comeback. The real problem is that your work reaches decision-makers through someone else. Fix the pipe.

- Send a short written summary *before* the meeting to the people who matter. "Here's where I landed on X, happy to walk through it Thursday." Now there's a timestamped record and you've framed it first.
- Ask to own the agenda item rather than having it relayed.
- Build a direct line to whoever needs to know. If your manager only hears about your work secondhand, that's the actual vulnerability.

**The direct conversation**

Worth having once, privately, low-drama:

*"In Thursday's meeting, the roadmap analysis came across as yours. That was my work. I'd like us to be clearer about attribution going forward."*

Then stop talking. Someone who did it carelessly will be embarrassed and correct it. A repeat offender will get defensive, tell you you're being sensitive, or explain how it was really a team effort. Either way you've learned what you're dealing with, and you've established that you noticed — which alone deters a lot of people.

**Escalating**

If it continues, go to your manager, but frame it around visibility rather than grievance. "I want to make sure you've got a clear picture of what I'm contributing on X" plays very differently from "Dave keeps stealing my credit." The first is a professional asking for accurate accounting; the second sounds like a personality conflict, and managers avoid those. Bring specifics: what you did, when, what got presented.

**The honest part**

Sometimes the manager already knows and doesn't care, because the credit-taker is useful to them. If you fix your visibility, have the direct conversation, escalate cleanly, and nothing changes — that's not a failure of your tactics. It's information about where you work, and it's worth taking seriously rather than grinding against for another two years.

Also worth internalizing: doing good work and making sure the right people know you did it are two different skills. The second one isn't self-promotion or politics. It's just part of the job, and the people who take credit understand that better than the people they take it from.
