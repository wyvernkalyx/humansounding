A few things would change my answer, but the biggest one is hiding in how you phrased it: a *second* developer means you currently have one. If software is core to what you sell, that's a single point of failure. One person quits, gets sick, or burns out and you're stuck — and contractors don't really solve that, because the context leaves when they do.

So the questions I'd want answered:

**Is the work permanent or a spike?** If you can see 12+ months of steady work, an employee is almost always cheaper per hour and compounds in value. If it's a six-month push or an unpredictable queue, contractors let you turn the spend off.

**Is it core or peripheral?** The stuff that differentiates your product — the domain logic, the architecture decisions — you want that knowledge accumulating in-house. A one-off integration, a mobile app you'll rarely touch, a design system: fine to contract.

**Who's managing them?** Contractors need more specification, not less. If your existing developer becomes a full-time requirements-writer and code reviewer, you've bought yourself less capacity than you think. This is the most common way the contractor math quietly fails.

**How fast do you need someone?** Contractors start in a week or two. A good hire takes two to four months end-to-end, plus a ramp-up period where they're net-negative on output.

**What's your cash situation?** Contractors are opex you can stop. An employee is a commitment with severance implications. If runway is tight or revenue is lumpy, that flexibility is worth real money.

My default lean: **if you have a year of visible work and software is your product, hire.** The all-in cost of an employee usually lands 30–50% below a comparable contractor rate, and you're buying institutional knowledge and redundancy, not just hours.

The pattern I'd actually suggest, though: use a contractor *now* to clear the immediate backlog while you run a hiring process. That buys you time to hire well instead of hiring fast, and you'll learn a lot about what you actually need from watching what the contractor spends their time on.

Two failure modes to watch for. Hiring someone junior because it's what the budget allows, when your one developer has no capacity to mentor — that makes things worse for six months. And the contractor treadmill, where you keep extending three-month engagements for two years and end up paying employee-plus-premium rates for someone with no stake in the codebase.

What's the work look like, and how long is your runway?
