**Short answer: it's a half-truth — closer to "heavily marketed by" than "invented by."**

**The history**

The earliest documented print use of the exact phrase does come from the cereal orbit: a 1917 article in *Good Health* magazine by dietitian Lenna Cooper stated that "in many ways the breakfast is the most important meal of the day." *Good Health* was edited by John Harvey Kellogg of the Battle Creek Sanitarium — brother of Will Keith Kellogg, who founded the cereal company. So the phrase emerged from the health-reform movement that also produced breakfast cereal, and cereal advertising (Kellogg's, Post's Grape-Nuts) then hammered it into the culture for decades.

But it wasn't conjured out of nothing. The idea that a working person needed a substantial morning meal was ordinary folk wisdom long before, and similar sentiments appear in 19th-century writing. Marketers amplified an existing belief rather than fabricating one.

A related story often gets mixed in: Edward Bernays' 1920s PR campaign for Beech-Nut, which surveyed physicians to endorse "hearty" breakfasts and helped cement bacon and eggs as an American ritual. That was genuinely manufactured demand — but for pork, not cornflakes.

**The science**

The "most important meal" claim doesn't hold up as a universal rule:

- Observational studies do link breakfast skipping to obesity, diabetes and heart disease — but skippers also tend to smoke more, drink more, exercise less and eat worse overall. The confounding is severe.
- Randomized trials are much less impressive. The Bath Breakfast Project found breakfast doesn't "kickstart your metabolism." A 2019 *BMJ* meta-analysis found that adding breakfast produced slightly *more* total daily calories and marginal weight gain, with no compensatory reduction later in the day.
- Dhurandhar's 2014 RCT found telling dieters to eat or skip breakfast made no difference to weight loss.

Where breakfast does look genuinely valuable:

- **Children and adolescents**, especially food-insecure ones — school breakfast programs show real benefits for concentration and attendance.
- **Meal timing generally.** A growing body of chrononutrition research suggests front-loading calories earlier in the day gives better glycemic control than eating heavily at night. That's an argument for not eating late, which isn't quite the same as an argument for breakfast, but they overlap.
- Anyone who finds skipping it leads to worse choices later, or who has medical reasons (diabetes medication, pregnancy, high training loads).

**Bottom line:** the slogan is marketing-inflected and the evidence doesn't support treating breakfast as uniquely essential for healthy adults. But the fashionable counter-claim that it's pure cereal-company invention overstates things too. Eat it if it works for you; skip it if it doesn't.
