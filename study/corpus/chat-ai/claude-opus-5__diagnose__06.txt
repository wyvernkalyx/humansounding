Most likely these aren't two problems. A meeting ends on time when items close; items close when someone makes a call and it gets written down. Overrun is evidence of non-closure, and the amnesia is downstream of the same thing. So the question is: why aren't things closing?

**The differential**

*Nothing was actually decided.* The most common cause of "nobody remembers what was decided" is that no decision occurred — there was a convergence of mood that four people left interpreting four different ways. Telltale sign: the same topic reappears in three consecutive weeks, each time treated as new. If this is it, the ambiguity is often serving a purpose — it lets people avoid a disagreement they don't want to have out loud.

*Decision rights are undefined.* Nobody knows who owns the call, so the group defaults to seeking consensus, which is slow and doesn't hold. Sign: discussions that trail off into "let's think about it more" or "let's circle back" without anyone naming what would resolve it.

*The agenda is a list of topics, not questions.* "Q3 roadmap" has no end condition, so it expands to fill available time. "Do we cut feature X or slip the date two weeks?" has one. Sign: item one eats forty minutes and the last three get compressed into the final five — which is also exactly when your most important decisions are getting made badly and recorded vaguely.

*Status reporting is crowding out deciding.* If half the meeting is people reading out things that could have been a paragraph in a doc, decisions get pushed to the tired end.

*Cold start.* No pre-read, so the first ten minutes of every item is people building context in real time, out loud, badly.

*Capture is nobody's job, or it's the facilitator's job.* Whoever is running the conversation cannot also be recording it. And notes that arrive Thursday for a Monday meeting arrive after the memory has already decayed.

**A test you can run next week**

At each apparent decision point, say out loud: "So — decision is X, owner is Y, done by Z," and type it on a shared screen where everyone can see it.

This is a diagnostic instrument, not just a discipline. Watch what happens:

- If you can write the sentence easily and the meeting speeds up — you just had a capture problem, and you've fixed it.
- If you try to write it and the room immediately starts amending it — you had a false-consensus problem. The sentence forced it into the open. Good; this is the fix working, even though it feels like it's making the meeting worse.
- If you can't write it because nobody can say who the owner is — decision rights. Escalate that as its own issue outside the meeting.
- If someone objects to writing things down as too rigid or bureaucratic — take that seriously as data. Occasionally it's genuine (early exploratory discussion shouldn't be minuted). Often it means someone benefits from the vagueness.

**The structural fixes, in order of leverage**

Put decisions first on the agenda and updates last, or move updates async entirely. Rewrite every agenda item as a question with a proposed answer attached — the proposer's job is to arrive with a recommendation, not an open topic. Name a scribe who isn't the facilitator. Circulate the decision log within an hour, not a day; it should be short enough that this is trivial, because it contains decisions and owners only, not a transcript. Open each meeting by reading last week's decisions aloud — thirty seconds, and it makes relitigating visible.

If it would help, tell me what the meeting is actually for, who's in the room, and roughly how the time splits between updates and discussion. The diagnosis sharpens a lot with those — in particular, whether you have a process problem or a conflict-avoidance problem wearing a process problem's clothes.
