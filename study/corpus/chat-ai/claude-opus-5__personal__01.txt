The one move I'd rule out is flat denial. Beyond the honesty problem, it's tactically terrible: if you later want to use the offer as leverage, you've already been caught lying about it, and if word gets back another way — competitor moves surface in small industries constantly — you've destroyed the trust permanently over something that wasn't even a fireable issue.

But honest doesn't mean full disclosure. You don't owe your employer a running commentary on your job search. There's a lot of room between lying and handing over the offer letter.

**First, quickly read why they're asking.** If they name the company or seem to already know, you're confirming something, not revealing it — vagueness will read as evasion. If it's a general "are you happy here?" fishing question, you have far more room to stay high-level.

**Then figure out which situation you're in, because the script changes:**

*If you'd genuinely stay for the right package:* Be direct and orient it toward staying. "I wasn't looking, but I got approached and it went further than I expected. I'd rather be straight with you — I do have an offer. My preference is to stay, but I want to have a real conversation about [comp / scope / title / the thing that's actually bothering you]."

*If you've basically decided to leave:* "I've been approached and I'm considering my options. I'm not ready to say more yet, but you'll hear it from me first and I won't leave you in a bad spot." Then don't drag it out — a long gap between this and your resignation reads as bad faith.

*If you genuinely don't know:* "Someone reached out — I wasn't looking for it. I haven't made any decisions. When I have something concrete, you'll hear it from me directly." Then decline to go further: "I'd rather not get into specifics until I know where I stand" is a legitimate, honest answer.

**Prepare for the immediate follow-up.** They will probably ask "who?" or "what would it take?" Have an answer ready. On "who," you can decline. On "what would it take," don't improvise — know your number and terms before you walk in, because this conversation can move fast.

**Two risks worth knowing:**

Going to a *competitor* specifically is the case where some companies walk you out the same day, especially in sales or anything with client relationships. Check your contract and think about whether you can afford that outcome before you confirm anything.

Even if they keep you, disclosing marks you as a flight risk with some managers — less investment in you, first considered in a cut. That doesn't mean stay quiet, but weigh it against how much you trust this particular boss.

And the standard caution on counteroffers holds: if the reason you interviewed was something money doesn't fix, more money usually doesn't fix it. Worth knowing what you'd actually be saying yes to.
