A predictable, recurring window is actually good news — it's reproducible and usually has a single dominant cause. The first fork in the road is: **is this load, or is something running on a schedule?**

## Step 1: Define "slow" precisely

Before hunting, pin down:

- **Which requests?** All pages, or specific endpoints (login, dashboard, search)?
- **How slow, at which percentile?** p50 doubling means something different from p99 spiking while p50 is flat (the latter usually means contention, locks, or a subset of unlucky requests).
- **Server-side or end-to-end?** Compare your server's request duration to real-user monitoring. If server timings are flat but users are slow, look at the network path, CDN, or client-side assets.
- **Is it 9am in one timezone, or 9am local for each user?** If it tracks a single wall-clock time regardless of where users are, that points to something in your infrastructure. If it follows users' local mornings, it's a load pattern.
- **How long does it last?** 5 minutes vs. 90 minutes tells you a lot.

## Step 2: Overlay traffic and latency graphs

This is the highest-value 10 minutes you'll spend. Pull up requests/second and response time for the 8:00–10:00 window over the last couple of weeks.

- **Latency rises *with* traffic** → capacity or scaling problem.
- **Latency rises *before* or *without* traffic** → something scheduled is competing for resources.
- **Traffic is flat but latency spikes** → almost certainly a background job, cache expiry, or an external dependency.

## If it looks like a scheduled job

Inventory everything that fires around that time:

- Cron jobs, systemd timers, Kubernetes CronJobs, Lambda schedules
- Nightly/morning batch imports, ETL, report generation, invoice runs
- Backups and snapshots (these can hammer disk I/O)
- Database maintenance: vacuum/analyze, index rebuilds, statistics updates
- Log rotation, antivirus/security scans, patch windows
- Cache or CDN TTLs that expire on a daily boundary
- Partner/vendor systems pushing you a daily file

Check the database specifically for long-running queries, lock waits, and blocked sessions during the window. A batch job holding locks or saturating I/O will make normal queries crawl even at modest traffic.

## If it looks like a load spike

Common causes for a business-hours ramp:

- **Login storm.** Password hashing (bcrypt/argon2) is deliberately CPU-expensive. Hundreds of simultaneous logins can pin your CPUs. Session creation and permission-loading on first request add to it.
- **Cold caches.** If your app caches are empty overnight (or you deploy at 3am), 9am is the first real traffic hitting cold. Everything falls through to the database at once.
- **Autoscaling lag.** Scale-up isn't instant — metric collection, scaling decision, instance boot, health checks, JIT warmup. You can easily be 5–15 minutes behind a sharp ramp.
- **Connection pool exhaustion.** Requests queue waiting for a DB connection. Look for pool wait time as a distinct metric; it's often invisible if you only measure query duration.
- **Third-party dependencies.** Your SSO/identity provider, payment gateway, or analytics vendor is also busiest at 9am. Check the latency of your outbound calls separately.
- **N+1 queries and unbounded queries** that are tolerable at low concurrency and catastrophic at high concurrency.

## Step 3: Get a trace from inside the window

Metrics tell you *when*; traces tell you *where*. If you have an APM tool (Datadog, New Relic, Honeycomb, Sentry, OpenTelemetry + Jaeger), pull a few slow traces from 9:05am and compare them to the same endpoint at 2pm. The extra time will be sitting in one place: a specific query, an external HTTP call, GC pauses, or queueing before your code even runs.

If you don't have tracing, this is the incident worth installing it for.

## Quick mitigations to consider once you know the cause

- Reschedule or throttle batch jobs; move them off peak or onto a replica
- Pre-warm caches with a synthetic job at 8:30
- Use **scheduled** scaling to pre-scale before 8:45 rather than reactive autoscaling
- Move expensive work off the request path into a queue
- Add a read replica for reporting queries
- Raise connection pool limits (carefully — the database has its own ceiling)
