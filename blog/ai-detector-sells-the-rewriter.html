The A.I. Detector That Also Sells the Tool for Beating It

How this was written: the Grammarly test, the measurements and the argument are mine. Claude drafted the prose and I approved it.

On Sept. 26 I pasted a block of text into Grammarly's A.I. detector to see what a student sees. It came back clean. Zero percent.

Under that verdict sat a card headed "Before you turn it in," offering an A.I. Rewriter that would "swap out phrases that may appear to be AI-generated." The detector had just finished telling me that nothing appeared to be AI-generated.

I ran it again using the sample text Grammarly supplies on its own detector page. That scored 99 percent. Same card.

You do not need an account to see the arrangement. The detector's public page advertises "one-click rewrites with AI Rewriter" as a feature of the detector itself, and its own reading list ends with a link titled "How to Avoid AI Detection (Responsibly)."

In June, Superhuman, the name Grammarly has gone by since last year, bought GPTZero, a detector with more than 19 million users. Grammarly already sold an A.I. detector and an A.I. humanizer on the same domain. The humanizer's page says the tool "is not intended to bypass AI detectors."

If your organization wrote an A.I. policy in the last two years, it probably carries a sentence saying that submitted work may be screened for A.I. generation, and that a positive result can open a review. That clause is the only part of the policy making a factual claim about the world.

Grammarly's detector page says the product "Achieves 99% detection accuracy and ranks #1 on RAID's independent benchmark." RAID is a real benchmark, published at the Association for Computational Linguistics conference in 2024. Its authors write that they "tune the threshold for each model to yield a 5% false positive rate."

Five percent is one human document in twenty. Grammarly's page does not say what false positive rate sits behind its 99 percent, and an institution that lets a detector score open a proceeding has accepted whatever that rate is, against its own people, in writing.

RAID also draws its machine text from GPT-2 XL, GPT-3, ChatGPT, GPT-4, Cohere, MPT 30B, Mistral 7B, and LLaMA 2 70B. The newest of them shipped in 2023. Further down the same page that claims 99 percent, Grammarly writes that "AI detectors can get it wrong." Its support documentation goes further: the feature "is not 100% accurate and should not be used as a definitive assessment of whether AI-generated text is present." All three sentences describe the same software.

I build these tools, so I went and measured one.

Mine is a regex checker that looks for the patterns everyone argues about: em dashes, the word "delve," the not-just-X-but-Y construction. I ran it against 959 documents written by people and published before Nov. 30, 2022, the day ChatGPT opened to the public. About 1.35 million words of Substack essays, Medium articles, email newsletters, and blog posts from 2004.

It flagged 602 of them, which is 62.8 percent of a corpus written before the thing it detects existed.

The em dash is the tell everyone knows, and of my fifteen rules it is the only one whose rate on any model clears every human corpus I have. It clears for Claude alone, and it still fires on 469 of those 959 human documents. Pre-ChatGPT Medium articles run 3.83 em dashes per thousand words. Pre-2022 email newsletters run 1.05. Both corpora are human and the two ranges do not overlap, so there is no human rate to accuse anyone against. There is a rate above which a person writes more like an essayist than like a newsletter.

Those model figures come from a seeded run of Sept. 1 against the models of that moment. They have already moved. A study published Oct. 1 by the marketing firm Graphite, which had current models rewrite 10,000 pre-ChatGPT articles, reports Claude Opus 5.5 using the em dash 99 percent less often than Opus 5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro all but dropping it. Graphite sells marketing services, and a model rewriting a summary of an article is doing a different job from a model writing one, so I would not lean on its exact figures. The direction is harder to argue with. The tell everyone knew was tuned out within one release, and a policy clause written in 2024 is now pointed at punctuation the models have abandoned.

That leaves provenance, the only approach here not built on inference. Anthropic marks Claude's output two ways. Generated files carry a signed Content Credential under the C2PA standard. Text carries something different, an imperceptible watermark woven into the words, which Anthropic says may persist through some editing. The company names what breaks it: heavy editing, paraphrasing, translation, or mixing into other writing. Detection sits in a private preview that organizations must apply to, so a university or a human resources department cannot check a text watermark today unless Anthropic has let it in. A watermark that is found still does not tell you who wrote the sentences, because people use these models on prose they wrote themselves.

My own tools sit on the wrong side of that. The checker does verb-aware word swaps and phrase deletions, which is a close relative of the editing Anthropic lists as watermark-degrading, and I have never measured whether it does the job. I should know that about my own tools and I do not.

If you own one of these policies, one edit does most of the work. Strike the language that lets a detector score open a proceeding by itself, and replace it with a disclosure requirement. Ask people to say what they used. Disclosure is enforceable against conduct you can establish, and it does not require you to defend a false positive rate nobody has measured.

The corpus counts, the rules, and the measurement scripts are public, at github.com/wyvernkalyx/humansounding. One study in that repo, on whether people can tell human prose from machine prose, has a sample floor I set before any data came in, and its script will not report a result below it. If you run your own baseline and get different numbers than mine, I would like to see them.
